# Climate types — reference note

Spoken summary: Climate is the long-term pattern of warmth, water, and
seasonality that a vineyard lives in. Three patterns matter most.
Maritime climates are smoothed by the ocean: cooler summers, milder
winters, small day-to-night swings, and rain through the year, which
means slower ripening, acidity kept, and disease as the main risk.
Continental climates are inland and swingy: hot summers, cold winters,
large day-to-night swings, frost and vintage variation as the main
risks, and lower humidity. Mediterranean climates are dry in summer
and wet in winter: reliable ripening, high sugar, falling acidity,
and drought and heat as the main risks. On top of those patterns,
cool, moderate, warm, and hot describe how much ripening a climate
allows, and latitude, altitude, oceans, and day-to-night range modify
a site within its pattern.

## Cool, moderate, warm, hot

Cool climates barely ripen grapes. Wines are lighter, higher in acid,
lower in alcohol, and can taste green if ripening is incomplete.
Moderate climates ripen most varieties with balance. Warm climates
ripen easily, with higher sugar and alcohol and lower acid. Hot
climates risk losing freshness altogether and need varieties and
techniques that preserve it. These are tendencies, not grades:
a cool climate is not worse than a warm one, and a warm climate
is not better. Each suits different grapes and styles.

## Maritime

Mechanism: water stores and releases heat slowly, so the ocean
moderates temperature. Summers are cooler, winters milder, and the
difference between day and night is smaller. Rain is spread through
the year, not concentrated in one season.

Consequences: slower, steadier ripening; acidity retained; less
winter kill; more humidity and disease pressure in the growing
season; canopy management, airflow, and drainage matter a great deal.

Examples: Bordeaux, coastal California, parts of the Loire.

## Continental

Mechanism: far from moderating water, land heats and cools quickly.
Summers are hot, winters cold, and the difference between day and
night is large.

Consequences: good summer ripening and cool nights that can preserve
acidity, but winter cold that can damage vines, spring frost after
budburst, and high vintage variation. Lower humidity usually means
less constant disease than a maritime climate, but a cold winter or
a frost spring changes the year sharply.

Examples: Burgundy (continental), Alsace, Champagne
(cool and continental-influenced), Piedmont (continental influence
inland from the Mediterranean).

## Mediterranean

Mechanism: a seasonal rainfall pattern. Winters are mild and wet;
summers are warm to hot and dry. The vine's water comes largely in
winter, and summer is a long, dry ripening period.

Consequences: reliable ripening, high sugar and potential alcohol,
falling acidity, and flavour that can become ripe and warm. The main
risks are drought, heat, and sunburn. Water management, drought-
tolerant rootstocks, irrigation where allowed, and canopy shade
matter more than fighting summer rain.

Examples: southern Rhône, Provence, southern Spain, Sicily,
and parts of California with a wet-winter, dry-summer pattern.

## Modifiers — latitude, altitude, oceans, diurnal range

Latitude sets the baseline: higher latitude means lower sun angle
and a shorter, cooler growing season, but longer summer days.
Lower latitude means more warmth and a longer season.

Altitude cools with height, roughly six tenths of a degree Celsius
per hundred metres as a rule of thumb, varying locally. Higher sites
can ripen later and preserve acidity, and can sit above fog or frost
pockets — but they also face greater cold, wind, and frost risk in
exposed positions. Altitude alone does not protect from frost.

Oceans and lakes moderate temperature and can bring fog, humidity,
and rain, or, in a rain shadow, dryness on the lee side. A lake can
also create autumn fog that helps noble rot in sweet-wine regions.

Diurnal range is the difference between day and night temperature.
A large range — hot days, cool nights — helps ripening by day while
preserving acid by night, and can help colour and aroma. It is not
the same as fog. Morning fog burning off does not itself create
diurnal range, though a clear, dry night after a hot day does.

## Frost, rain, drought — the risk summary in sentences

Maritime climates are most at risk from rain-related disease in the
growing season, because humidity and rain through the year favour
fungal disease, and rain at harvest can dilute and rot fruit.
Continental climates are most at risk from spring frost, because
budburst is followed by cold nights and cold air pooling, and from
winter cold damage, because inland winters are severe. Mediterranean
climates are most at risk from summer drought and heat, because
summer rain is scarce and the long dry season stresses vines and
can sunburn fruit. Each pattern can suffer the other risks in a
given year, but these are the characteristic risks to plan around.
