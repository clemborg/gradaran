# Soil, water, and site — reference note

Spoken summary: Region gives you the climate, but site gives you
the vineyard's actual experience of it. Aspect is which way a slope
faces, and in the northern hemisphere south-facing slopes get more
sun and warmth. Slope drains water and cold air, so valley floors
collect both and can become frost pockets and waterlogged ground.
Soil affects water, vigour, and ripening, and through those, wine —
not by transferring flavour directly from rock to grape. And fog
can be a problem in one place and a tool in another, depending on
timing and purpose.

## Aspect, slope, and sun

In the northern hemisphere, south-facing slopes receive more direct
sun and warmth, and ripen earlier. North-facing slopes are cooler.
In the southern hemisphere, the pattern reverses: north-facing slopes
are warmer. In a hot climate, a cooler aspect can be an advantage,
because the goal shifts from achieving ripeness to preserving freshness.
Steep slopes can also capture sun at a better angle than flat land,
which is why steep, south-facing sites in cool climates (the Mosel,
the Côte d'Or) are prized.

## Slope, cold air, and frost pockets

Cold air is dense and flows downhill, like water. It pools in hollows
and valley floors, which can therefore be colder than mid-slope sites
on a frosty night, even though they are lower. A mid-slope site is
often safest from frost, because cold air drains past it. An exposed
summit can also be frost-prone, from wind and cold, but for a different
reason. Frost risk is therefore not simply higher with elevation,
and not simply lower with elevation. It depends on where cold air
flows and where it pools. This was a specific correction in the
November 3 session and should not be reintroduced as an error.

## Soil, water, drainage, and vigour

Soil affects wine mainly through water availability, drainage, root
depth, and vigour. Deep, water-retentive soils support vigorous leaf
growth and higher yields, and can dilute flavour in wet years.
Free-draining, poorer soils limit vigour, reduce yield, and can
concentrate fruit — if water does not become severely limiting.
Gravel soils drain well, warm up, and suit Cabernet Sauvignon on the
Left Bank of Bordeaux. Limestone soils drain well and limit vigour,
helping concentration in Burgundy and Chablis. Clay soils retain
water, stay cooler, and suit Merlot on the Right Bank. Slate soils
in the Mosel capture and retain heat, helping Riesling ripen in a
cool climate. In each case, the mechanism is water, warmth, and
vigour, not a direct transfer of mineral flavour.

## Root-zone oxygen

Roots need air as well as water. Waterlogged soil suffocates roots,
even if the vine is not thirsty. Good drainage is therefore about
air, not just about removing excess water. This distinction matters:
berry rot from humid, wet conditions is a different problem from
root-zone waterlogging, though both involve water. A well-drained
soil can still suffer berry rot in a wet, humid harvest, and a
waterlogged soil can damage roots even without berry rot.

## Fog — two stories

Coastal or river fog cools and delays ripening, and can bring disease
pressure, because humidity favours fungal disease. That is fog as a
problem. Autumn fog in a sweet-wine region — Sauternes and Barsac,
Tokaj — can promote noble rot, Botrytis cinerea, which shrivels
grapes, concentrates sugar and acid, and adds honey, apricot, and
spice flavours. That is fog as a tool. The difference is timing,
purpose, and whether the fog is followed by warm, dry afternoons
that allow noble rot rather than grey rot.

## Vintage, weather, and climate — in one sentence each

Climate is the long-term pattern over decades. Weather is this
season's conditions. Vintage is the year's weather, experienced
through a region's climate and a site's aspect, slope, and soil.
A single warm year does not make a region warm, and a single frost
does not make it continental.
