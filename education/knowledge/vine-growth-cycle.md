# Vine growth cycle — reference note

Spoken summary: A grapevine lives the same year every year, with
different weather. In winter it is dormant, with no leaves, storing
energy in roots and wood. In spring, buds burst, carrying shoots,
leaves, and flowers. Flowering is fragile: cold or rain can reduce
fruit set, which is when flowers become berries. Berries then grow,
and at véraison they start to ripen — red grapes colour, white grapes
soften and become translucent. After véraison, sugar rises, acid falls,
colour and tannin develop, and flavour builds. Harvest is a judgement
about all of those together, not a date. After harvest, leaves fall,
reserves rebuild, and dormancy returns.

## The stages, in order, with what happens

Dormancy: no leaves, no growth, energy stored. The vine needs a
cold rest for even budburst, and it is still vulnerable to extreme
winter cold, even without leaves.

Budburst: buds open, shoots and leaves emerge, and inflorescences
appear. Once buds burst, green tissue is vulnerable to frost.
A late spring frost after an early budburst can destroy shoots
and crop in a night.

Flowering: flowers open and must be pollinated and fertilised.
Warmth and calm help; cold, rain, and wind reduce set. Flowering
is not the same as fruit set.

Fruit set: fertilised flowers become berries. The berries that set
are the crop, and their number sets a ceiling on yield. Poor set
means small or uneven bunches and lower yield.

Berry growth: berries enlarge, mostly by cell division and then
by water uptake, while remaining hard, green, and high in acid.

Véraison: the turning point. In red grapes, skins colour. In white
grapes, berries soften and become translucent. Véraison signals
that ripening has begun; it is not ripening itself.

Ripening: sugar rises in the pulp, malic acid falls, colour and
tannin develop in skins and seeds, and flavour compounds accumulate.
This phase depends on warmth, sunlight, and water in the remaining
season.

Harvest: picking, when sugar, acid, flavour, and tannin maturity
are judged together. Pick too early and the wine is acidic without
flavour or mature tannin. Pick too late and it is high in alcohol
without freshness.

Leaf fall and reserves: after harvest, leaves keep photosynthesizing
for a while, rebuilding reserves for next spring. Cutting this short
weakens the next cycle, and the vine returns to dormancy.

## Why timing matters

Budburst timing affects what weather flowering meets. Flowering
weather affects set and yield. Véraison timing affects how much
warm season is left for ripening. That chain is why vintage is not
just a year label: it is the weather's fingerprint on this chain,
stage by stage.

## Vocabulary to get right

Véraison is spelled with an accent and pronounced roughly
vay-ray-ZOHN. It is the start of ripening, not ripeness itself.
Fruit set is the moment berries form, not flowering. Dormancy is
rest, not death. Harvest is a decision, not a calendar date.
