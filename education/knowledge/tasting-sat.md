# Tasting — Systematic Approach to Tasting (SAT)

Spoken summary: The Systematic Approach to Tasting is a discipline,
not a vocabulary test. You describe in order — appearance, nose,
palate, then conclusions — and you conclude from what you described,
not from a guessed label. Appearance is clarity, intensity, and colour.
Nose is condition, intensity, aroma characteristics, and development.
Palate is sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity
and characteristics, and finish. Conclusions are quality and readiness
for drinking or potential for ageing. Tasting runs in parallel with
theory throughout this syllabus. It does not replace theory, and theory
does not replace tasting. No formally assessed SAT blind-tasting note
is recorded yet, so SAT progress is not yet evidenced, only practised
through personal notes.

## Appearance — say what you see, not what you think it is

Clarity: clear, hazy, or faulty. Intensity: pale, medium, or deep.
Colour: for whites, lemon-green, lemon, gold, amber; for reds, purple,
ruby, garnet, tawny; for rosés, pink, salmon, orange. Do not name a
grape or region in the appearance section. A pale colour does not
mean a light wine in every case — Nebbiolo is pale and tannic, for
example — but appearance is still the first, objective observation.

## Nose — condition, intensity, aroma, development

Condition: clean or unclean (faults such as cork taint, oxidation,
reduction, volatile acidity, Brettanomyces — described, not diagnosed
beyond evidence). Intensity: light, medium, pronounced.
Aroma characteristics: primary (grape and fermentation: fruit, floral,
herbal), secondary (winemaking: oak, lees, malolactic), tertiary
(ageing: earth, leather, dried fruit, honey, petrol in aged Riesling).
Development: youthful, developing, fully developed, tired or past its
best. Development is judged from aroma, not from age alone.

## Palate — structure, then flavour, then finish

Sweetness: dry, off-dry, medium-dry, medium-sweet, sweet.
Acidity: low, medium, high. Tannin (reds): low, medium, high, and
ripe vs green in character. Alcohol: low, medium, high.
Body: light, medium, full. Flavour intensity: light, medium,
pronounced. Flavour characteristics: as for the nose, in the same
primary, secondary, tertiary order. Finish: short, medium, long.
Balance is the relationship between these elements: does acidity
balance alcohol and sweetness? Does tannin balance fruit? Length is
how long flavour persists after swallowing. Intensity is how strong
flavours are. Complexity is how many different, layered flavours there
are. These four — balance, length, intensity, complexity — are the
basis for a quality conclusion, not price or prestige.

## Conclusions — quality and readiness, from the description

Quality: faulty, poor, acceptable, good, very good, outstanding.
A good wine is clean, balanced, and expressive. A very good wine adds
intensity, length, and complexity. An outstanding wine adds all of
those plus a sense of place, precision, and ageing potential that is
rare. Readiness: drink now, drink now but will improve, can drink now
but has potential for ageing, too old. Readiness follows from structure:
a high-tannin, high-acid wine with primary fruit still dominant has
ageing potential; a wine whose fruit has faded and whose tertiary
notes dominate is fully developed or past its best.

## Blind tasting discipline

Describe first, conclude second. Do not write a region or grape in
the appearance, nose, or palate sections. If you guess a label, keep
it out of the description and check it afterwards against the
description, not against your hope. A correct label guess with a poor
description is not a pass. A good description with a wrong label guess
is still good tasting, because the conclusion should follow from the
description.

## Progression in this syllabus

Start with a structured white note and a structured red note, using
the full SAT order, with a wine whose label is known, so the
description can be checked. Then practise with a wine whose label is
hidden, and check the conclusion against the description. Then, and
only then, practise blind quality assessments, where the conclusion
is the test. Weekend tasting practice is optional and is not a
substitute for theory checkpoints. Personal tasting notes in the
cellar log are exposure, not SAT evidence, unless they are written
in SAT form and logged as such.
