# Sweetness, sparkling, and fortified terms — reference note

Spoken summary: Three vocabularies cause most confusion in this
syllabus, and all three are about labels, not about taste alone.
German sweetness is about ripeness at harvest, not sweetness in the
finished wine. Sparkling sweetness is about dosage after disgorging,
and Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut, which is the opposite of what
the words suggest. Fortified sweetness is about when spirit is added:
during fermentation, the wine stays sweet; after fermentation, it is
dry, unless sweet wine is added afterwards. If you learn those three
sentences, the rest of this note is detail.

## German sweetness and ripeness

Prädikat levels, in order of ripeness at harvest: Kabinett, Spätlese,
Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese, Eiswein. Higher Prädikat
means riper must, not necessarily sweeter finished wine, because
fermentation can go dry. Sweetness words: trocken means dry,
halbtrocken means off-dry, feinherb means off-dry in a looser,
producer-dependent sense. A Kabinett trocken is therefore a light,
dry wine, not a sweet one. A Spätlese can be dry or sweet, depending
on fermentation. Always read Prädikat and trocken together, not
Prädikat alone.

## Sparkling sweetness and dosage

Dosage is sugar solution added after disgorging, following lees
ageing in the traditional method. The scale, from driest to sweetest,
is: Brut Nature (0 to 3 grams per litre), Extra Brut (0 to 6),
Brut (0 to 12), Extra Dry (12 to 17), Sec (17 to 32), Demi-Sec
(32 to 50), Doux (over 50). Note that Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut.
This is historical terminology, not a logical scale, and it is a
frequent exam trap. Brut is the most common dry style.

## Fortified sweetness and timing

Fortification is adding grape spirit to raise alcohol. If spirit is
added during fermentation, yeast dies, sugar remains, and the wine
is sweet — Port is the classic example. If spirit is added after
fermentation, all sugar has already become alcohol, and the wine is
dry — dry Sherry and dry Madeira are examples. Sweet Sherry is made
by adding sweet wine or must (often Pedro Ximénez) to a dry Sherry
base, not by fortifying during fermentation. Fortification therefore
determines alcohol, not sweetness by itself. Timing determines
sweetness.

## Other terms that confuse

Noble rot (Botrytis cinerea, in its noble form) shrivels grapes,
concentrates sugar and acid, and adds honey, apricot, and spice
flavours, under foggy mornings and warm afternoons. Grey rot is the
same fungus in wet, humid conditions without that drying pattern,
and it damages grapes rather than concentrating them. The difference
is conditions, not a different organism.

Passerillage and appassimento both involve drying grapes, but
passerillage usually means drying on the vine (late harvest, with
grapes shrivelling naturally), while appassimento means drying after
harvest, on mats or in drying rooms, as in Amarone. Both concentrate
sugar, but the method and timing differ.

Vendanges Tardives means late harvest in Alsace, giving ripe, often
off-dry to sweet wines. Sélection de Grains Nobles means a selection
of noble-rotted berries, giving lusciously sweet wines. Both are
production categories, not simply sweetness levels, and sweetness
still varies by producer.

Puttonyos, in Tokaj, historically indicated the number of puttonyos
(baskets) of aszú berries added to a barrel of base wine, and therefore
sweetness and concentration. Modern Tokaji labelling uses residual
sugar, but the historical term still appears and should be understood
as a concentration indicator, not as a grape or a place.
