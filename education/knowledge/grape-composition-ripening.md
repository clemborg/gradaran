# Grape composition and ripening — reference note

Spoken summary: A grape is not one thing ripening, but several
things ripening at different speeds. Sugar in the pulp becomes
alcohol. Acids give freshness: malic acid falls sharply as grapes
warm and ripen, tartaric acid is more stable. Colour lives mostly
in skins, for reds. Tannin lives in skins and seeds, and its maturity
matters as much as its amount. Flavour compounds build late, and
they are what separate a grape that is sugar-ripe from a grape that
is flavour-ripe. A winemaker balances all of these together, and
that balance is set by climate, site, and the season's weather.

## The components, where they live, and what they do

Sugar: accumulates in the pulp during ripening. In fermentation,
yeast converts sugar to alcohol and carbon dioxide. More sugar at
harvest means higher potential alcohol, roughly. Warmth and sunlight
raise sugar; cool conditions and early harvest lower it.

Malic acid: sharp, green-apple acidity. It is respired away as
berries warm and ripen, so cool climates and cool nights preserve
it, and warm climates lose it. That is why a cool-climate white can
taste crisp at the same sugar as a warm-climate white that tastes soft.

Tartaric acid: the more stable acid. It falls less with ripening,
so it provides a floor of acidity even in warm regions. A wine's
total acidity is not one acid, and the malic-tartaric distinction
explains many cool versus warm differences.

Colour: in red grapes, colour compounds (anthocyanins) live in
skins. White grapes have little skin colour to extract. Skin
contact during fermentation is therefore what makes a red wine red.

Tannin: in skins and seeds, and, if used, stems. Tannin gives
structure, bitterness, and a drying sensation. Unripe tannin tastes
green, bitter, and harsh. Ripe tannin tastes softer and more
integrated, even at the same quantity. Colour and tannin extraction
in red winemaking depends on skin and seed maturity, not just on
how long skins stay in contact.

Flavour compounds: build late in ripening, in skins and pulp, and
vary by variety and climate. They are the hardest to measure and
the easiest to lose by picking too early or too late. A grape can
be sugar-ripe and still lack flavour ripeness.

## Water stress — mild versus severe

Mild water stress after véraison can be beneficial: smaller berries,
more skin relative to juice, more concentration of colour, tannin,
and flavour. Severe water stress stops ripening, shuts the vine down,
and can damage leaves and fruit. The difference is degree and timing,
not a rule that stress is always good or always bad. Stress before
véraison has different effects from stress after it, because berry
growth and ripening are different phases.

## Ripeness is a judgement, not a number

A high sugar reading alone does not mean a ripe grape. A winemaker
is balancing sugar against acid, tannin, and flavour, and the right
balance depends on the wine being made: sparkling base wine is picked
early, high in acid and low in sugar, on purpose; a late-harvest sweet
wine is picked late, high in sugar, on purpose; a red for long ageing
needs tannin maturity that a light red for early drinking does not.
Ripeness is therefore relative to intention, not absolute.
