# History & Fun Facts — WSET Level 3

> **Use at most 1–2 facts per session, only where they advance the narrative, never as trivia for its own sake.**
>
> Each fact below is tied to a syllabus point. If a fact does not help the learner understand
> a Level 3 concept in that session, leave it out. Do not add dates or figures that are not
> stated here, and do not present any of these as exam answers on their own — they are
> illustrations, not substitutes for the syllabus.

---

## Chapter 1 — Natural Factors: Viticulture

### 1. Qvevri winemaking in Georgia is ancient
Winemaking in large clay vessels called qvevri, buried in the ground, has been practised in
Georgia for a very long time — around 8,000 years, as commonly cited. The vessels are still
used today, including for amber (skin-contact) wines.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It shows that winemaking long predates modern tanks and
oak, and that vessel choice (clay, oak, steel) is a human decision that shapes flavour
and texture — a theme that returns in Chapter 3.

### 2. Phylloxera reshaped viticulture worldwide
Phylloxera, a tiny root louse, devastated European vineyards in the 19th century. The
solution that saved viticulture was grafting European vines (Vitis vinifera) onto
resistant American rootstocks — the practice still used for most of the world's vines today.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It explains why grafting and rootstock choice are part of
viticulture, why some ungrafted vines are now rare and prized, and why vineyard pests
and disease are a core natural-factor topic.

### 3. The Cistercian monks mapped Burgundy by site
Cistercian monks in Burgundy observed, over generations, which vineyard plots consistently
gave the best wine, and recorded those differences by site. That close observation of
place is an early form of the site-based thinking behind Burgundy's vineyard hierarchy.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It is the historical root of terroir and of Burgundy's
village / Premier Cru / Grand Cru thinking in Chapter 4 — quality linked to a specific
piece of land, not just to grape or winemaker.

## Chapter 2 — Human Factors

### 4. The 1855 Bordeaux Classification ranked estates by price
For the 1855 Classification of the Médoc, estates were ranked into growths largely
according to the price their wines commanded. Price was taken as a proxy for quality
and reputation at the time.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It explains why the Classification is a commercial
ranking frozen in history, not a tasting of today's wines — and why its structure
still shapes Bordeaux prices and labels in Chapter 5.

### 5. Mouton Rothschild was promoted to First Growth in 1973
Château Mouton Rothschild was the only estate ever promoted within the 1855
Classification, rising to First Growth in 1973. Its motto, in translation, is
"First I was, second I shall not be" — a reminder of how long it campaigned for the change.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It is the single exception that proves the rule in
Fact 4: the 1855 Classification has otherwise barely changed, so learners should
not expect it to reflect current quality rankings.

## Chapter 3 — Winemaking and Maturation

### 6. The traditional method makes bubbles in the bottle
In the traditional method, a second fermentation takes place inside the bottle itself,
trapping carbon dioxide as bubbles, and the wine then ages on its lees (dead yeast cells).
That lees ageing is what gives traditional-method wines their biscuit and brioche notes.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It connects method to flavour in Chapter 15: lees time
is not a detail, it is the reason Champagne tastes different from a tank-method sparkling wine.

## Chapter 4 — Burgundy, Chablis and Beaujolais

### 7. Burgundy's monks tasted the difference between neighbouring plots
See Fact 3 above — the Cistercians' plot-by-plot records are why Burgundy is taught
as the classic example of site mattering more than grape variety alone: the same
Pinot Noir or Chardonnay tastes different from one vineyard to the next.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It gives learners a reason to learn Burgundy's
geography in the first place, rather than memorising village names as a list.

## Chapter 5 — Bordeaux and South-West France

### 8. Bordeaux's Left Bank / Right Bank divide is a river divide
The Gironde estuary and its two rivers, the Garonne and the Dordogne, split Bordeaux
into the Left Bank (west) and the Right Bank (east). The gravel soils of the Left Bank
suit Cabernet Sauvignon; the clay and limestone of the Right Bank suit Merlot.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It turns a map into a tasting prediction — grape,
soil and bank together explain why Left Bank and Right Bank Bordeaux taste different.

## Chapter 8 — Germany, Austria and Tokaj

### 9. Tokaj's sweetness comes from noble rot (aszú)
In Tokaj, grapes affected by noble rot (Botrytis cinerea) shrivel and concentrate,
and those aszú berries are used to make the region's famous sweet wines. The rot
must be the "noble" form, in the right conditions — the same fungus in the wrong
conditions is simply grey rot and ruins grapes.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It is the clearest example of how a vineyard
hazard, under the right climate, becomes a winemaking asset — and it anchors
the sweet-wine and botrytis vocabulary for the exam.

### 10. German Prädikat measures ripeness, not sweetness alone
A German wine labelled Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese was made from grapes picked at
a defined level of ripeness. The same Prädikat wine can be fermented dry (trocken)
or left sweet — the word on the label tells you about the grapes, not the final sweetness.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It corrects the most common German-wine mistake:
assuming a higher Prädikat always means a sweeter wine in the glass.

## Chapter 9 — Italy

### 11. Etna's vineyards grow on a live volcano
Mount Etna, on Sicily, is an active volcano, and its vineyards grow on volcanic soils
at altitude. That combination — volcanic soil, altitude, and a Mediterranean climate —
is central to Etna Rosso's character.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It is a compact case study in natural factors:
soil type, altitude and climate together, not any one factor alone, explain a wine's style.

## Chapter 13 — South Africa

### 12. Pinotage was crossed in South Africa in 1925
Pinotage was created in South Africa in 1925 as a cross between Pinot Noir and
Cinsault (then known locally as Hermitage — hence "Pinot-age"). It is South Africa's
signature red grape.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It shows that grape varieties are not all ancient:
new crossings are part of viticultural history, and a country's signature grape can
be a 20th-century creation, not a medieval inheritance.

## Chapter 15 — Sparkling Wine

### 13. Champagne's bubbles come from a second fermentation, not from invention by one person
The traditional method — second fermentation in bottle, then lees ageing — developed
over time through the work of many winemakers. The popular story that Dom Pérignon
invented Champagne is a myth and should not be taught.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It keeps the focus on the method itself, which is
what the exam tests: second fermentation, lees ageing, and how they create
Champagne's flavour and mousse.

## Chapter 16 — Fortified Wine

### 14. Port is fortified to stop fermentation and keep sweetness
Port is made by adding spirit to fermenting must, which stops fermentation and leaves
residual sugar in the wine, while raising its alcohol. That is why Port is both
sweet and strong.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** Fortification is a winemaking intervention with a
direct, predictable result — sweetness plus higher alcohol — and Port is the
textbook example of it.

### 15. Sherry's flor is a living layer of yeast
In the production of fino-style Sherry, a layer of yeast called flor grows on the
surface of the wine in barrel, protecting it from oxygen while it ages. That biological
ageing is what gives fino its pale colour and distinctive savoury character.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It contrasts biological ageing (under flor) with
oxidative ageing (without flor) — a core fortified-wine distinction in Chapter 16.

## Across Chapters — Labelling and Style

### 16. Old World wines are often labelled by place, New World wines by grape
A Bordeaux or a Chianti is usually labelled by where it comes from, with the grape
implied by that place; a Californian or Australian wine is more often labelled by
grape variety. Neither system is a quality ranking — both are labelling traditions.

**Why it matters for Level 3:** It helps learners read a label and predict what
is inside, and it stops the "Old vs New" divide being treated as a quality judgement.
