# Key grapes — reference note

Spoken summary: A grape is best learned by what it needs and what
it gives, not by a list of flavours alone. Pinot Noir needs a cool
climate and a good site, and gives delicate, high-acid, low-tannin
wine that ages into earth and mushroom notes. Cabernet Sauvignon
needs warmth and time, and gives structured, tannic, blackcurrant
wine that ages for decades. Chardonnay is versatile: it takes on
climate and winemaking, from lean Chablis to rich, oaked Meursault.
Nebbiolo needs a long, warm autumn, and gives pale, high-tannin,
high-acid wine that is harsh young and complex with age. Riesling
keeps its acid in cool climates and makes wines from dry to sweet.
Grenache ripens easily and gives warm, alcoholic, soft wine that
needs blending for structure. Syrah gives dark, peppery, tannic wine,
and Tempranillo gives cherry, plum, and, with oak and age, vanilla
and leather. The rest of this note gives each grape the same treatment:
needs, gives, and what goes wrong.

## Pinot Noir

Needs: cool to moderate climate, a site that ripens it fully without
losing acidity, and protection from frost, rain, and rot, because
its skins are thin. Gives: light to medium body, high acidity,
low to medium tannin, red fruit (cherry, raspberry, strawberry),
and, with age, earth, mushroom, and forest-floor notes. What goes
wrong: picked early, it is green and thin; picked late in a warm
climate, it loses freshness and becomes jammy.

## Chardonnay

Needs: a climate that matches the intended style — cool for lean,
high-acid styles, warm for ripe, full styles. Gives: a neutral base
that reflects climate and winemaking more than a strong varietal
character: citrus and green apple when cool and unoaked, stone fruit
and tropical notes when warm, butter and vanilla when it sees
malolactic fermentation and new oak. What goes wrong: over-oaked
Chardonnay loses its fruit; underripe Chardonnay in a very cool
climate can be thin and green.

## Cabernet Sauvignon

Needs: warmth, time, and a free-draining site, preferably gravel.
Gives: full body, high tannin, blackcurrant, cedar, and, with age,
tobacco, leather, and cigar notes. What goes wrong: underripe
Cabernet tastes green and herbaceous, with harsh tannin; it needs
a warm season to mature tannin, not just to accumulate sugar.

## Merlot

Needs: a slightly cooler or more water-retentive site than Cabernet,
because it ripens earlier. Gives: medium to full body, softer tannin
than Cabernet, plum and red fruit, and earlier approachability.
What goes wrong: in a very warm climate, Merlot can lose acidity
and become soft and flat; it needs a site that preserves freshness.

## Nebbiolo

Needs: a long, warm autumn, hillside sites, and fog — the name is
linked to fog in Piedmont. Gives: pale colour despite high tannin
and acid, rose, tar, cherry, and, with age, leather and dried fruit.
What goes wrong: picked early, Nebbiolo is harsh and green-tannined,
even if sugar is high. Tannin maturity lags sugar, so patience matters
more than for most grapes.

## Sangiovese

Needs: warmth to ripen, but a site that preserves its high acidity.
Gives: medium body, high acid, medium tannin, cherry and herbal notes.
What goes wrong: in a poor site or vintage, Sangiovese can be thin
and tart; in a warm site with low yields, it can be structured and
ageworthy, as in Brunello.

## Syrah / Shiraz

Needs: warmth to ripen fully, with a site that preserves freshness.
Gives: dark fruit, black pepper, and, with age, savoury, meaty, and
olive notes, with medium to high tannin. What goes wrong: underripe
Syrah is peppery and lean in an unripe way; overripe Syrah in a very
hot climate becomes jammy and alcoholic.

## Grenache / Garnacha

Needs: warmth and sun; it ripens easily. Gives: warm, ripe, red fruit,
high alcohol, low tannin and colour, and spice. What goes wrong:
alone, Grenache can lack structure and colour, which is why it is
usually blended with Syrah and Mourvèdre in the southern Rhône and
with other varieties in Spain.

## Tempranillo

Needs: a climate that ripens it fully without losing freshness —
Rioja's cooler subzones and Ribera's altitude both suit it.
Gives: cherry, plum, and, with oak and age, vanilla, coconut (from
American oak), leather, and tobacco. What goes wrong: in a very warm
site, Tempranillo can become soft and jammy; in a cool site, it can
be thin.

## Riesling

Needs: a cool climate, with slope and sun to ripen it at all in
marginal sites. Gives: high acidity, aromatic citrus, green apple,
stone fruit, and floral notes, and, with age, honey and petrol-like
notes. Capable of dry to lusciously sweet styles. What goes wrong:
in a warm climate, Riesling loses its defining acidity and delicacy.

## Chenin Blanc

Needs: a climate that allows its high acidity to survive ripening.
Gives: high acid, honey, quince, and apple notes, capable of dry,
off-dry, sweet, and sparkling styles. What goes wrong: without
enough ripeness, Chenin can be tart and lean; without acidity,
sweet Chenin becomes cloying.

## Sauvignon Blanc

Needs: a cool to moderate climate to balance its aromatic intensity
with acidity. Gives: grassy, gooseberry, citrus, and, in warm
climates, tropical notes, with high acidity. What goes wrong: too
cool and it is green and harsh; too warm and it loses freshness
and becomes flat.

## Gamay, Pinotage, Malbec, and others in brief

Gamay: light, fruity, high-acid red from Beaujolais, capable of
serious cru wines from granite sites, not only Nouveau.
Pinotage: a South African crossing of Pinot Noir and Cinsault,
dark and fruity when ripe, harsh and rubbery when underripe.
Malbec: dark, plum, violet, and chocolate, structured at altitude
in Mendoza, cooler and more tannic in Cahors.
Sémillon: waxy, honeyed, low-acid white, central to Sauternes
blends and to Hunter Valley ageing whites.
