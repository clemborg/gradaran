## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Italy makes more wine, from more grapes, in more climates, than any other country in this syllabus — and that is both its fascination and its difficulty. The way through it is not to memorise every grape, but to follow a few logics: north is cooler and more continental, south is warmer and more Mediterranean; Nebbiolo and Sangiovese are high-acid, high-tannin grapes that need time and warmth to mature; Veneto has a unique drying technique, appassimento, that concentrates grapes before fermentation; and the islands, especially Etna, rewrite what 'southern' means, because altitude creates cool sites inside a warm island. Piedmont is the best starting point, because Nebbiolo's demands — late ripening, high tannin and acid, fog in the hills — explain Barolo and Barbaresco more clearly than any list of appellations could.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Nebbiolo: late ripening, thin skins but high tannin and acid, pale colour, rose, tar, cherry, and, with age, leather and dried fruit. It needs a long season and a warm autumn; picked early, it is harsh and green-tannined. Barolo and Barbaresco differ mainly in site and ageing rules: Barolo is more structured and longer-aged; Barbaresco is slightly earlier-ripening and more approachable young, though both age well.
- Sangiovese: high acid, medium tannin, cherry and herbal notes, sensitive to site and clone. In Chianti Classico it is blended with other varieties; in Brunello di Montalcino it is 100% Sangiovese (as Brunello, a local clone) and aged longer, giving a more structured, ageworthy wine.
- Appassimento: grapes dried after harvest, losing water and concentrating sugar, acid, and flavour, then fermented. Amarone is dry, full, and high in alcohol from dried Corvina and partners; Ripasso is a Valpolicella refermented on Amarone pomace, giving extra body and ripeness without Amarone's cost.
- Etna: volcanic soils, altitude (often 600-1000 m), and old bush vines create cooler, later-ripening sites on a Mediterranean island. Nerello Mascalese makes pale, elegant, Burgundy-like reds; Carricante makes high-acid, mineral whites. Altitude, not latitude, is the key.
- Southern Italy: warm, Mediterranean, with grapes like Aglianico (high tannin and acid, ageworthy, in Taurasi and Aglianico del Vulture) and Nero d'Avola (warm, ripe, plum and spice in Sicily) that need heat to ripen but must preserve freshness, often via altitude or coastal influence.

## Concrete examples

- Barolo vs Barbaresco: same grape, neighbouring zones, different soils, exposures, and ageing rules — a practical comparison for how site and law shape style.
- Amarone vs Valpolicella Classico: same grapes, one dried and one not — the clearest demonstration of appassimento's effect on alcohol, body, and flavour.
- Chianti Classico vs Brunello: Sangiovese in two forms — blended and earlier-drinking vs single-variety and long-aged — showing how appellation rules and site change the same grape.
- Etna Rosso: a Mediterranean island wine that tastes cool, pale, and elegant, because altitude and volcanic soil override the island's warmth.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Barolo and Barbaresco are the same wine.' No: different zones, soils, and ageing rules, and Barbaresco is generally more approachable young, though both are Nebbiolo and both age.
- 'Amarone is sweet.' Amarone is dry (though rich and ripe, which can feel sweet). The sweetness impression comes from concentration and alcohol, not residual sugar — except in Recioto, the sweet version.
- 'Super Tuscans are a legal category.' No: Super Tuscan is a nickname for high-quality Tuscan wines made outside DOC/G rules, often with international varieties, originally labelled as table wine. It is a historical and stylistic label, not an appellation.
- 'Southern Italian wine is all hot and simple.' Aglianico and Etna wines can be structured, fresh, and ageworthy, especially at altitude.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. Why does Nebbiolo need a long, warm autumn, and what happens if it is picked early?
2. How does appassimento change a grape, and why does Amarone taste rich without being sweet?
3. What distinguishes Barolo from Barbaresco, in site and in law?
4. Why does Etna taste cooler and more elegant than its Sicilian latitude would suggest?
5. How does Sangiovese change between Chianti Classico and Brunello di Montalcino?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If Nebbiolo is treated as just 'tannic': teach tannin maturity separately from tannin amount, and ask why a young Barolo can be harsh even though its tannin is 'high quality' in principle.
- If Amarone sweetness confuses: separate residual sugar from ripe fruit impression and alcohol warmth, and test with a dry Amarone vs a sweet Recioto.
