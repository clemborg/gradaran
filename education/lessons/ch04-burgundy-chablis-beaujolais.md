## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Burgundy is small, complicated, and worth the effort, because it makes the link between place and wine more explicit than almost anywhere else. The region grows mainly two grapes — Pinot Noir for reds, Chardonnay for whites — in a cool, semi-continental climate where ripening is marginal and vintage matters enormously. Because the grapes are constant, differences between wines are mostly differences of place: soil, slope, aspect, and the exact site. That is why Burgundy classifies vineyards, not just wines: a village wine, a premier cru, and a grand cru are, in principle, the same grapes from better and better sites. In practice, producer matters as much as classification — a great producer's village wine can outshine a careless producer's premier cru — and that tension between place and producer is the heart of understanding Burgundy.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Cool climate + Pinot Noir: Pinot ripens early but is thin-skinned and sensitive to rot, frost, and rain. It needs a site that ripens it fully without losing acidity, and a vintage that allows it. That is why Burgundy vintages vary so much.
- Limestone and slope: the Côte d'Or is a limestone escarpment, east-facing, with mid-slope sites prized for drainage, sun, and frost protection. Limestone soils drain well and limit vigour, helping concentration.
- Classification as a site hierarchy: grand cru sites are the most consistently well-placed; premier cru are excellent but slightly less so; village wines come from the commune's other sites; regional wines from anywhere in Burgundy. The hierarchy describes potential, not a guarantee in any given bottle.
- Chablis: the coolest part of Burgundy, with Kimmeridgian limestone and clay. Chardonnay here is typically lean, high in acid, with green apple, citrus, and mineral notes, and often unoaked — though some producers use oak, especially at premier and grand cru level.
- Beaujolais: Gamay on granite in the north (the crus) gives structured, ageworthy reds; Gamay on clay in the south gives lighter, fruitier wines. Carbonic maceration — fermenting whole bunches in carbon dioxide before crushing — gives the bubblegum, banana, and bright red-fruit character of Nouveau, and, used more gently, freshness in cru wines.

## Concrete examples

- Côte de Nuits village vs premier cru: the same producer, the same vintage, different sites — a practical way to taste what classification actually means, and where it stops meaning anything.
- Chablis vs Côte de Beaune Chardonnay: Chablis is leaner, higher in acid, and more mineral; Meursault or Puligny-Montrachet is rounder, richer, and more likely to see oak and MLF. Same grape, different climate and winemaking.
- Morgon vs Beaujolais Nouveau: Morgon, a cru, can age and develop savoury, structured character; Nouveau is made to drink within months, bright and simple. Both are Gamay; the difference is site, method, and intent.
- Mature Burgundy: the user's preference for mature, tertiary Burgundy makes sense here — Pinot Noir with age develops earth, mushroom, and forest-floor notes as primary fruit fades, and the wine's value shifts from fruit to complexity.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Grand cru always means better than premier cru.' No: classification is about site potential. Producer, vintage, and bottle condition can overturn it in a single tasting.
- 'Chablis is always unoaked.' No: many Chablis are unoaked, but premier and grand cru Chablis often see some oak. 'Chablis style' is a tendency, not a rule.
- 'Beaujolais is all light and simple.' No: cru Beaujolais (Morgon, Moulin-à-Vent, Fleurie, and others) can be structured and ageworthy. Nouveau is the exception, not the definition.
- 'Burgundy is only about Pinot Noir.' Chardonnay is half the region, and white Burgundy — from Chablis to Montrachet — is as central as red.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. Why does Burgundy classify vineyards rather than grape varieties?
2. How does a cool, semi-continental climate shape Pinot Noir differently from a warm climate?
3. What distinguishes Chablis Chardonnay from Côte de Beaune Chardonnay, and why?
4. What is carbonic maceration, and what character does it give Gamay?
5. Why can a village wine from a great producer outshine a premier cru from a poor one?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If classification is treated as a quality guarantee: use a single producer, single vintage, village vs premier cru comparison and ask what changed — site, not grape or method.
- If Chablis is reduced to 'unoaked Chardonnay': introduce oaked premier cru Chablis and ask what oak adds and what it risks in a cool, high-acid wine.
