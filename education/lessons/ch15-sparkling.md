## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Sparkling wine is not a grape or a region; it is a method, and the method is the story. In the traditional method, the second fermentation happens in the bottle, the wine ages on lees, and the result is complex, bready, and fine-bubbled — but expensive and slow. In the tank method, the second fermentation happens in a large pressurised tank, the wine is filtered and bottled under pressure, and the result is fresh, fruity, and affordable — ideal for aromatic grapes like Glera in Prosecco. Transfer method is a middle path: bottle fermentation, then transfer to tank for disgorging. Carbonation is the simplest: injecting carbon dioxide, giving large, short-lived bubbles. Champagne is the most famous traditional-method wine, but it is not the only one, and its rules — grapes, chalk, dosage, vintage vs non-vintage — are what make it Champagne, not just the bubbles.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Traditional method: base wine (high acid, low alcohol, neutral) + tirage (yeast and sugar) → second fermentation in bottle → CO2 trapped → lees ageing (autolysis: bready, biscuity, toasty notes, finer mousse, more complexity) → riddling and disgorging (removing lees) → dosage (sugar solution determining sweetness: Brut Nature, Extra Brut, Brut, Extra Dry, Sec, Demi-Sec, Doux).
- Tank method: second fermentation in tank, lees contact brief, no autolysis complexity, fresh fruit preserved, cost lower, bubbles slightly coarser and less persistent. Suits aromatic, fruity grapes.
- Transfer method: bottle fermentation for complexity, then transfer under pressure to tank, disgorging and bottling — a compromise between traditional complexity and tank efficiency.
- Dosage and sweetness: dosage is added sugar after disgorging. Brut is the most common dry style (0-12 g/L residual sugar); Extra Brut and Brut Nature are drier; Extra Dry is, confusingly, slightly sweeter than Brut; Demi-Sec is sweet. Sweetness labels in sparkling wine do not follow still-wine logic.
- Champagne climate and chalk: cool, marginal, continental-influenced, with chalk soils that drain well, retain warmth, and give high-acid base wines ideal for sparkling. Non-vintage Champagne blends years and vineyards for house style and consistency; vintage Champagne is from a single, declared year and ages longer.

## Concrete examples

- Champagne non-vintage vs vintage: NV is a house blend, consistent, aged at least 15 months on lees; vintage is a single year, aged at least 3 years, more complex and ageworthy — a lesson in blending vs vintage expression.
- Blanc de Blancs vs Blanc de Noirs: Blanc de Blancs (100% Chardonnay) is typically lighter, more citrus and floral; Blanc de Noirs (Pinot Noir/Meunier) is fuller, more red-fruit and structured. Same method, different grapes.
- Prosecco vs Champagne: Prosecco (Glera, tank method) is fresh, fruity, pear and floral, best young; Champagne (traditional method) is complex, bready, and ageworthy. The difference is method and grape, not just price.
- Crémant d'Alsace / Cava: traditional-method wines from other regions, with local grapes (Cava: Macabeo, Xarel-lo, Parellada), showing that traditional method is not exclusive to Champagne.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Champagne is the only sparkling wine.' No: Champagne is a place and a method. Crémant, Cava, Franciacorta, and English sparkling use the same method in other places.
- 'Extra Dry means drier than Brut.' No: in sparkling labelling, Extra Dry (12-17 g/L) is sweeter than Brut (0-12 g/L). The terminology is historical, not logical.
- 'Prosecco is just cheap Champagne.' Prosecco is a different method, grape, and intention: fresh and fruity, best young, not a failed Champagne.
- 'Bubbles mean quality.' Mousse quality (fine, persistent bubbles) relates to method and lees ageing, but a wine can be bubbly and simple, or still and great. Bubbles are a feature, not a quality score.

5. Why is Champagne's cool climate and chalk soil suited to sparkling base wine?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If methods blur: teach them as a spectrum of time, cost, and lees contact — traditional (long, expensive, complex) → transfer (middle) → tank (short, fresh, affordable) → carbonation (instant, simple) — and test with an unfamiliar sparkling wine's label.
- If dosage confuses: write the dosage scale in order, with grams per litre, and ask the learner to place Brut, Extra Dry, and Demi-Sec on it before adding tasting notes.
