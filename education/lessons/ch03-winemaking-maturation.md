## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Winemaking is often taught as a recipe — crush, ferment, age, bottle — but it is better taught as a series of choices about extraction and oxygen. White wine is usually made by pressing grapes and fermenting juice without skins, so colour and tannin stay low. Red wine is fermented with skins, so colour, tannin, and flavour are extracted over days. Rosé is a deliberate middle path: brief skin contact for colour, then fermentation like a white. Everything after fermentation — whether malolactic fermentation happens, whether the wine sits on lees, whether it sees oak, how much oxygen it meets — continues that same logic: what to keep from the grape, what to soften, and what to add. A winemaker who understands that logic can explain why two wines from the same grape taste nothing alike.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Fermentation: yeast converts sugar to alcohol and carbon dioxide, releasing heat. Cool fermentation preserves fresh, fruity aromas in whites; warmer fermentation extracts more colour and tannin in reds and can lose delicate aromas if too hot.
- Extraction in reds: colour and tannin come from skins (and, if used, stems). Longer, warmer maceration extracts more; gentler methods extract less. Punching down and pumping over manage the cap of skins and control extraction and temperature.
- Pressing: free-run juice is generally finer than press juice, which is more tannic and phenolic. Press fractions are a quality decision, not just a yield decision.
- Malolactic fermentation (MLF): bacteria convert sharp malic acid to softer lactic acid, reducing acidity and adding creamy, buttery notes (diacetyl). It is usually wanted in reds and in some full whites, and usually avoided in crisp whites where freshness is the point.
- Lees: dead yeast cells after fermentation. Lees ageing (sur lie) adds texture, body, and sometimes bready notes, and protects against oxidation. Stirring lees (bâtonnage) increases the effect.
- Oak: new oak adds vanilla, spice, toast, and tannin, and allows slow oxygen ingress that softens tannin. Used oak adds mainly oxygen and texture, with little flavour. Oak is a seasoning, not a quality guarantee: too much oak masks fruit.
- Maturation and bottle ageing: red wines with tannin, acid, and concentration can develop tertiary aromas (leather, earth, dried fruit) and soften with time. Most whites and light reds are made to drink young, and ageing them does not improve them.

## Concrete examples

- Champagne base wine vs still Chardonnay: the same grape, but base wine is picked early, high in acid, and fermented cool and clean — a winemaking choice driven by the sparkling goal.
- Burgundy Pinot Noir vs Beaujolais: both Pinot/Gamay reds, but extraction, whole-bunch use, and oak choices create very different tannin and aroma profiles.
- Oaked vs unoaked Chardonnay: MLF and new oak together create a buttery, vanilla, full style; stainless steel and blocked MLF create a crisp, fruit-driven style. The grape is the same; the decisions are not.
- Barolo: long maceration and ageing in large oak (botti) or barrique, reflecting Nebbiolo's high tannin and acid and the need for time to soften it.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Oak means quality.' No: oak is a tool. Used well, it adds complexity; used heavily on a light wine, it overwhelms it. Many great wines see no new oak at all.
- 'Malolactic fermentation is always good.' No: it softens acid. In a crisp white, that softness is a loss, not a gain.
- 'Longer ageing always improves wine.' No: only wines with the structure (tannin, acid, concentration) to develop benefit. Most wines are best young.
- 'Natural wine means no winemaking.' No: all wine involves winemaking decisions, including the decision to intervene less. 'Low intervention' is a choice about which tools to use, not an absence of choice.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. Why is white wine usually pressed before fermentation, while red wine is fermented with skins?
2. What does malolactic fermentation change, and why would a winemaker want it in a red but block it in a crisp white?
3. How does new oak differ from used oak, in flavour and in oxygen?
4. Why does a high-tannin red often improve with bottle age, while a light white usually does not?
5. What is the difference between free-run and press juice, and why does it matter?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If fermentation is treated as magic: re-teach it as sugar → alcohol + CO2 + heat, then ask what temperature control is actually controlling.
- If oak is treated as a quality label: compare the same grape oaked and unoaked and ask what changed — flavour, texture, oxygen, cost — and what was lost.
