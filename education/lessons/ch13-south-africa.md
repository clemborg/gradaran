## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

South Africa has grown wine since the 17th century, but its modern reputation was remade after apartheid, when investment, replanting, and a focus on site and old vines changed what the country could make. The Western Cape is a Mediterranean climate with a twist: the Atlantic and Indian oceans, the Benguela Current's cooling, and the Cape Doctor wind (a strong south-easterly) cool and dry coastal sites, while inland valleys heat up. That contrast — cool Walker Bay and Elgin vs warm Paarl and Swartland interior — is the chapter's engine. Chenin Blanc, called Steen locally, is the most planted white and the most versatile, from crisp to sweet. Pinotage, a South African crossing, is the most debated grape: when ripe and well-made, it is dark, fruity, and smoky; when underripe or over-extracted, it can be harsh and rubbery. Old vines, especially in the Swartland, are a separate story: dry-farmed bush vines, low yields, and concentrated, savoury wines that have become South Africa's most distinctive modern style.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Coastal cooling: ocean currents and wind cool coastal sites, preserving acidity and delaying ripening. Inland sites, shielded from wind, are warmer and riper.
- Cape Doctor wind: a strong, dry south-easterly that reduces humidity and disease, but can also damage vines and dry soils if too strong. Wind is a viticultural factor, not just weather.
- Chenin Blanc versatility: high acid, capable of dry, off-dry, sweet, and sparkling styles, with honey, quince, and apple notes. In South Africa, old Chenin vines give concentration that young vines do not.
- Pinotage origin and character: a 1925 crossing of Pinot Noir and Cinsault, designed to combine Pinot's quality with Cinsault's heat tolerance. Its character — dark fruit, banana (from isoamyl acetate, especially with carbonic influence), smoke, and earth — depends heavily on ripeness and winemaking.
- Old-vine Swartland: dry-farmed, bush-vine, unirrigated sites with deep roots, low yields, and concentrated fruit. The style is savoury, structured, and often blended (Syrah, Grenache, Chenin, and others) rather than single-variety.

## Concrete examples

- Walker Bay Pinot Noir/Chardonnay vs Paarl Cabernet: coastal cool vs inland warm, from the same province, showing South Africa's range.
- Swartland Syrah/Chenin blends: old-vine, dry-farmed, savoury, and structured — a modern South African signature that is not about a single grape.
- Constantia: historic sweet wines (Vin de Constance, Muscat-based) from a cool, coastal site, with a history stretching to the 18th century — a reminder that South African sweet wine predates its modern red reputation.
- Stellenbosch Cabernet: structured, dark, and ageworthy, from a warmer, inland site with good soils and a long growing season.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Pinotage always tastes burnt and rubbery.' No: those notes are often a sign of underripeness or over-extraction. Ripe, well-made Pinotage is dark, fruity, and balanced.
- 'South African wine is all warm and heavy.' Coastal sites (Walker Bay, Elgin, Constantia) are cool and elegant, with high-acid whites and Pinot Noir.
- 'Old vines always mean better wine.' Old vines can give concentration and depth, but only if they are healthy and well-managed. Age alone does not guarantee quality.
- 'Chenin Blanc is a simple grape.' In South Africa, old-vine Chenin makes some of the country's most complex, ageworthy whites, from dry to sweet.
