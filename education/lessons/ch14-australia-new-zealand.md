## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Australia is a continent, not a climate, and its wines range from tropical Hunter Valley Semillon to cool Tasmanian Pinot Noir. The famous styles — Barossa Shiraz, Margaret River Cabernet, Clare Riesling — each come from a specific climate logic, not from 'Australia' as a whole. New Zealand is the opposite: a small, cool, maritime country where latitude, ocean, and wind dominate, and where Sauvignon Blanc from Marlborough became a global style almost overnight: pungent, tropical, high in acid, and unmistakable. Central Otago, the world's southernmost wine region, makes Pinot Noir in a continental, high-altitude, large-diurnal-range climate that is nothing like Marlborough's. Together, these two countries test whether a learner can hold a continent's variety and an island's specificity in the same chapter without reducing either to a stereotype.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Australian climate range: Mediterranean in the south-west (Margaret River), warm continental in Barossa and McLaren Vale, humid subtropical in Hunter Valley, cool in Yarra and Tasmania. Each region's style follows its climate, not a national template.
- Barossa Shiraz: warm, old vines, full, ripe, dark fruit, chocolate, and spice, high in alcohol. Old vines and low yields concentrate fruit, but warmth alone would make it jammy without structure from tannin and oak.
- Margaret River Cabernet: maritime, Mediterranean, with gravel soils and ocean moderation, giving structured, elegant Cabernet with blackcurrant and cedar — a Bordeaux-like logic in an Australian climate.
- Hunter Semillon: humid, warm, with Semillon picked early, low in alcohol, high in acid, and capable of long ageing, developing toast and honey with time — a counterintuitive ageing story for a light white.
- Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc: cool, sunny, maritime, with long daylight hours, giving pungent, tropical, passionfruit and gooseberry aromas, high acidity, and a crisp, dry style. The pungency comes from thiols, developed in cool, sunny conditions.
- Central Otago Pinot Noir: continental, high altitude, large diurnal range, with hot days and cold nights, giving ripe, dark, structured Pinot with cherry and thyme notes, and good ageing potential.

## Concrete examples

- Barossa Shiraz vs Yarra Pinot Noir: warm, full, and powerful vs cool, elegant, and high-acid, from the same country — a lesson in Australia's range.
- Margaret River Cabernet vs Bordeaux Cabernet: similar maritime-gravel logic, different hemisphere and winemaking, with Margaret River often riper and more fruit-forward.
- Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc vs Sancerre Sauvignon Blanc: pungent and tropical vs restrained and mineral — same grape, opposite climate and winemaking emphases, and a prior gap to revisit explicitly.
- Central Otago Pinot Noir vs Martinborough Pinot Noir: continental-altitude vs maritime-cool, both New Zealand Pinot, different structures.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Australian wine is all big Shiraz.' No: Australia makes cool-climate Pinot, Riesling, and Chardonnay, and Hunter Semillon is light and ageworthy. Barossa is famous, not typical.
- 'Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc is the only New Zealand wine.' Central Otago Pinot, Hawke's Bay Syrah and Chardonnay, and Martinborough Pinot are equally important at Level 3.
- 'New Zealand is all cool and maritime.' Central Otago is continental, with hot summers and cold winters, and a large diurnal range — the exception that proves the maritime rule for the rest of the country.
- 'Hunter Semillon cannot age because it is light.' Hunter Semillon is one of the world's great ageing whites, developing toast, honey, and complexity over decades from a light, high-acid start.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. Why does Australia not have a single national style, and what are its main climate logics?
2. How does Marlborough's cool, sunny, maritime climate create its Sauvignon Blanc style?
3. Why is Hunter Semillon ageworthy despite being light and low in alcohol?
4. How does Central Otago differ from Marlborough, and what does that mean for Pinot Noir?
5. Why is Margaret River Cabernet often compared with Bordeaux, and where does the comparison break down?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If Marlborough is the only New Zealand taught: introduce Central Otago as the counter-example, and ask the learner to place both on a maritime-continental spectrum before adding Hawke's Bay and Martinborough.
- If Australian Shiraz is treated as one style: compare Barossa (warm, old-vine, full) with a cool-climate Shiraz (Yarra or Canberra) and ask what changed — climate, not grape.
