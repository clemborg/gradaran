## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Germany is the coolest major wine country in this syllabus, and that coolness is the whole story. Riesling ripens slowly, keeps its acidity, and can make wines from bone dry to intensely sweet, all from the same grape, depending on when it is picked and how fermentation is handled. German labelling tries to tell you that story, but it does so by ripeness at harvest — must weight — not by sweetness in the finished wine, and that distinction confuses almost everyone at first. Austria, next door, shares some grapes and a continental climate but labels differently, with its own DAC system and a famous white pepper note in Grüner Veltliner. Tokaj, in Hungary, takes the sweet end further: grapes shrivelled by noble rot, picked berry by berry, and made into Aszú wines that balance intense sweetness with high acidity and can age for decades.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Cool climate + Riesling: slow ripening preserves acid and builds delicate aromas (citrus, green apple, stone fruit, floral, and with age, petrol-like notes from TDN). Low alcohol is a consequence of coolness, not a flaw.
- Must weight and Prädikat: Kabinett is the lightest, earliest-picked style; Spätlese is later-picked and riper; Auslese is selected ripe bunches; Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese are botrytised selections; Eiswein is frozen on the vine. Higher Prädikat means riper must, not necessarily sweeter finished wine — fermentation can go dry.
- Sweetness vs ripeness: a Spätlese can be fermented dry (trocken) or left sweet. The label's sweetness words (trocken = dry, halbtrocken = off-dry) and the producer's style matter as much as the Prädikat level.
- River and slope effects: the Mosel's steep, south-facing slate slopes capture sun and heat in a cool climate, and the river moderates frost and reflects light. Without those slopes, Riesling would often not ripen at all.
- Tokaj aszú: Furmint (and Hárslevelü) affected by noble rot, shrivelled, picked as aszú berries, macerated in must or wine, and fermented slowly. The number of puttonyos historically indicated sweetness/concentration; modern labelling uses residual sugar. Acidity from Furmint balances the sweetness.

## Concrete examples

- Mosel Kabinett: light, low in alcohol (often 8-9%), high in acid, off-dry or dry, with citrus and slate character — the classic correction to the idea that Kabinett always means sweet.
- Rheingau Spätlese: riper, fuller, often with stone fruit and honey notes, capable of dry or sweet styles depending on fermentation.
- Wachau Grüner Veltliner: white pepper, green apple, citrus, with a savoury, food-friendly character; the Wachau's own categories (Steinfeder, Federspiel, Smaragd) indicate ripeness and alcohol, not a formal quality hierarchy in the same way as Prädikat.
- Tokaji Aszú: honey, apricot, orange peel, and spice, with piercing acidity — a sweet wine that is not cloying, and that ages for decades.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Kabinett means sweet.' No: Kabinett describes ripeness at harvest. A Kabinett can be dry (trocken), off-dry, or sweet, depending on fermentation.
- 'German wine is all sweet.' No: much German wine is dry, and trocken labelling exists precisely to say so. The sweet reputation comes from exported styles, not from the whole country.
- 'Petrol notes mean a fault.' In aged Riesling, petrol-like aromas from TDN are a normal, often prized, development, not a fault — though excessive TDN in a young wine can be a flaw of overexposure.
- 'Tokaj is a grape.' Tokaj is a place; Furmint is the main grape. Tokaji Aszú is a wine made there, by a specific noble-rot method.

3. How do Mosel slopes and the river help Riesling ripen?
4. What is the difference between a trocken Spätlese and a sweet Spätlese?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If Prädikat and sweetness blur: make a two-column distinction — ripeness at harvest vs residual sugar after fermentation — and test with two wines of the same Prädikat, one dry, one sweet.
- If Mosel is treated as 'cold = bad': teach slope, river, and slate as heat-capture tools, and ask what would happen on flat land in the same climate.
