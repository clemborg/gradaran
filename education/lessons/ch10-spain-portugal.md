## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Spain is mostly hot and dry, but its best wines often come from altitude, Atlantic influence, or long ageing — three ways of escaping the heat. Rioja, the most famous region, is not one climate: Rioja Alta is cooler and more Atlantic, Rioja Alavesa is higher and more continental, Rioja Oriental is warmer and more Mediterranean, and Tempranillo and Garnacha behave differently in each. Rioja's ageing categories — Crianza, Reserva, Gran Reserva — are legal minimums for time in barrel and bottle, and they shape style as much as grape does: vanilla and spice from American oak, softened tannin from time, and dried fruit and leather with age. Portugal, next door, is Atlantic in the north (Vinho Verde's light, high-acid whites) and warmer inland (the Douro's steep, terraced reds), with indigenous grapes that are rarely seen elsewhere, which is exactly why they must be learned by logic, not by translation to French grapes.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Tempranillo: early ripening, medium tannin, cherry, plum, and, with oak and age, vanilla, coconut (from American oak), leather, and tobacco. It suits Rioja's cooler subzones and Ribera's altitude, where it ripens fully without losing freshness.
- Rioja ageing law: Crianza requires at least two years total, with at least one in oak; Reserva at least three years, with at least one in oak; Gran Reserva at least five years, with at least two in oak. These are minimums; producers often exceed them. Ageing softens tannin, adds oak character, and develops tertiary aromas.
- American vs French oak: American oak gives stronger vanilla, coconut, and dill notes and is traditional in Rioja; French oak gives subtler spice and toast. Oak choice is a style decision, not a quality ranking.
- Ribera altitude: vineyards at 700-1000 m, with large diurnal range, ripen Tempranillo (Tinto Fino) fully while preserving acid, giving structured, dark, ageworthy wines.
- Priorat licorella: slate soils that are poor, steep, and heat-retaining, with old, low-yield Garnacha and Cariñena vines, giving concentrated, mineral, powerful reds.
- Vinho Verde: Atlantic, cool, wet, with high acid, light body, low alcohol, and often a slight spritz — a climate wine, not a grape wine, though Albariño (Alvarinho) in the north makes more serious, aromatic whites.

## Concrete examples

- Rioja Crianza vs Gran Reserva: same region, different ageing — a practical lesson in what time in oak and bottle actually does to tannin, fruit, and aroma.
- Ribera del Duero vs Rioja: same grape, different altitude and climate — Ribera is typically darker, more structured, and more modern in oak use.
- Priorat: a small, steep, slate region making powerful reds from old vines — a contrast to Rioja's larger, more varied production.
- Vinho Verde Alvarinho vs Douro red: Atlantic lightness vs inland structure, from the same country, showing Portugal's range.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Rioja is a grape.' No: Rioja is a place. Tempranillo is the main grape, with Garnacha, Graciano, and others in blends.
- 'Gran Reserva always means better.' No: Gran Reserva means longer ageing by law. A great Crianza from a top producer can outshine a weak Gran Reserva, and Gran Reserva style (more tertiary, less primary fruit) is not to everyone's taste.
- 'Spanish wine is all warm and heavy.' Rioja Alta, Rías Baixas, and Vinho Verde are cool, Atlantic, and fresh. Spain's range is wider than its southern heat.
- 'Portuguese grapes are just Spanish grapes with different names.' Some overlap (Tempranillo/Tinta Roriz, Garnacha/Tinta Francisca), but many Portuguese varieties (Touriga Nacional, Alvarinho, Encruzado) are distinct, and the Douro's field blends are a tradition of their own.
