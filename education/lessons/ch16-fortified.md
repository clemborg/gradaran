## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Fortified wines are wines with added spirit, and the single most important fact about them is when that spirit is added. Add spirit during fermentation, and fermentation stops, leaving residual sugar — the wine is sweet. Add spirit after fermentation, and the wine is dry, because all the sugar has already become alcohol. That timing distinction explains Port vs Sherry in one sentence: Port is fortified during fermentation and is sweet; most Sherry is fortified after fermentation and is dry, with sweet Sherry made by adding sweet wine or must afterwards. After fortification, maturation takes over: Port ages in bottle or in wood, Sherry ages in a solera under flor or oxidatively, and those two ageing logics create two completely different families of wine from a similar starting idea.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Fortification timing: spirit (usually 77% grape spirit) added during fermentation kills yeast, stops sugar conversion, and leaves a sweet, high-alcohol wine (Port, sweet Muscat). Added after fermentation, it raises alcohol without leaving sugar (dry Sherry, dry Madeira).
- Port production: Douro Valley, steep terraced vineyards, field blends of Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz, and others, fermented with skins, fortified during fermentation, then aged. Ruby Port ages briefly in large vessels, retaining fruit and colour; Tawny Port ages longer in small barrels, oxidising slowly, losing colour, gaining nutty, caramel, dried-fruit notes. Vintage Port is from a single declared year, aged briefly in wood then long in bottle, and needs decanting. LBV is from a single year, aged longer in wood, filtered, and ready to drink.
- Sherry production: Jerez, Palomino for dry styles, Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel for sweet. Flor is a layer of yeast that grows on Fino/Manzanilla in barrel, protecting the wine from oxygen and giving a distinctive yeasty, saline, almond character. The solera system blends wines of different ages, so a Sherry has no vintage — it is a fractional blend, with the oldest wine drawn off and replaced by younger wine. Oxidative styles (Amontillado, Oloroso) age without flor, gaining nutty, caramel, and dried-fruit notes.
- Madeira: heated (estufagem) and oxidised, making it virtually indestructible after opening — a unique maturation story, with styles from dry Sercial to sweet Malmsey, by grape.
- Serving and ageing: fortified wines are high in alcohol (15-22%), so they are served in smaller measures. Vintage Port ages for decades in bottle; Tawny is ready when bottled; Fino should be drunk fresh; Oloroso and Pedro Ximénez can age in bottle after opening longer than most wines, but Fino cannot.

## Concrete examples

- Ruby vs Tawny Port: same starting wine, different ageing — Ruby is fruity, dark, and young; Tawny is nutty, pale, and oxidised. The difference is wood time and oxygen, not grape.
- Fino vs Oloroso Sherry: Fino ages under flor, protected from oxygen, pale, dry, and yeasty; Oloroso ages oxidatively, darker, nuttier, and fuller. Same region, opposite ageing logics.
- Vintage Port vs LBV: Vintage is bottled young and ages in bottle, needing decanting and decades; LBV is aged longer in wood, filtered, and ready to drink — a practical distinction for buying and serving.
- Madeira: a wine that survives heating and oxidation, and can be kept open for months — the exception to almost every wine-storage rule.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Port and Sherry are the same kind of wine.' No: Port is sweet (fortified during fermentation) and from the Douro; Sherry is mostly dry (fortified after fermentation) and from Jerez, with a solera and flor system Port does not use.
- 'Tawny Port is a lower quality than Vintage Port.' No: Tawny and Vintage are different styles, not quality ranks. A great 20-year Tawny is not a failed Vintage.
- 'Sherry has a vintage.' No: solera Sherry is a fractional blend of many years, so it has no vintage year. Vintage-dated Sherry is the exception, not the rule.
- 'Fortified wine is all sweet.' Dry Sherry (Fino, Manzanilla, Amontillado, Oloroso) and dry Madeira (Sercial) are dry. Fortification determines alcohol, not sweetness — timing does.

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If fortification timing blurs: teach it as a single timeline — fermentation → spirit added during = sweet, after = dry — and test with Port, dry Sherry, and sweet Sherry on that timeline.
- If solera confuses: describe it as a fractional blend, not a barrel, and ask what happens when the oldest wine is drawn off and replaced.
