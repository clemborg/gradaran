## Lesson 1.1 — The vine and the annual cycle

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, the learner can, without prompting:

- Name the vine's structural parts and what each does: green growth
  (shoots, leaves, tendrils), one-year-old wood (the canes left after
  pruning), and permanent wood (trunk and cordons).
- Walk through the annual cycle in order and say what is happening
  at each stage, not just its name: dormancy, budburst, flowering,
  fruit set, véraison, ripening, harvest, and the return to dormancy
  (leaf fall).
- Explain why timing matters: an early or late event changes what
  weather the vine meets later, and therefore ripening and risk.

### Introduction (the 1–3 minute opening, in text)

A grapevine is a perennial plant that does the same job every year
with a different weather report. In winter it is dormant: no leaves,
no growth, energy stored in roots and permanent wood. That dormancy
is not laziness; it is how a temperate plant survives cold it cannot
run away from.

When spring warmth accumulates, buds burst. Those buds are not just
leaves — they carry the shoots, leaves, and inflorescences that will
become this year's crop. Flowering follows, and it is fragile: cold,
rain, or wind at flowering can reduce fruit set, because a flower
that is not pollinated and fertilised does not become a berry.
Fruit set is the moment the vine commits: the berries that survive
set are the crop, and their number sets a ceiling on yield.

Then comes a long, quiet phase of berry growth, followed by
véraison — the visible turning point. In red grapes the skins colour;
in white grapes the berries soften and become translucent. Véraison
is not ripening itself; it is the signal that ripening has begun.
After véraison, sugars rise, acids fall (malic acid especially),
colour and tannin develop in skins and seeds, and flavour compounds
accumulate. Harvest is a judgement, not a date: pick too early and
you have acidity without flavour or tannin maturity; pick too late
and you have sugar and alcohol without freshness. After harvest,
leaves fall, the vine stores reserves, and dormancy returns.

The whole cycle is therefore a chain. Budburst timing affects
flowering weather. Flowering weather affects set and yield.
Véraison timing affects how much of the warm season is left for
ripening. That chain is why "vintage" is not a vague word for
"year" — it is the weather's fingerprint on that chain.

### Core mechanisms, causally

- **Dormancy and chilling:** the vine needs a cold rest. Without
  it, budburst is uneven. In very warm climates, insufficient
  dormancy can itself be a problem.
- **Budburst → frost risk:** once buds burst, green tissue is
  vulnerable. A late spring frost after an early budburst can
  destroy the year's shoots and crop in a night. This is why
  budburst date, site, and frost protection matter together.
- **Flowering → set:** flowering needs warmth and calm. Cold and
  rain cause poor set, small or uneven bunches, and lower yield.
  A variety that flowers early meets different weather than one
  that flowers late, in the same vineyard.
- **Véraison → ripening window:** véraison starts the clock.
  Everything after it — sugar, acid loss, colour, tannin, flavour —
  depends on warmth, sunlight, and water in the remaining season.
  A late véraison in a cool climate leaves little room before
  autumn cold or rain.
- **Leaf fall and reserves:** after harvest, leaves keep
  photosynthesizing for a while, rebuilding reserves for next
  spring. Cutting this short weakens the next cycle.

### Concrete examples

- **Burgundy, Pinot Noir:** early budburst and early ripening
  make Pinot sensitive to spring frost and to autumn rain. A
  frost year or a wet harvest year shows up directly in yield
  and in ripeness.
- **California, coastal vs inland:** the same variety can bud
  and ripen weeks apart depending on fog, ocean influence, and
  inland heat. That is climate, not variety, doing the work.
- **Champagne:** a cool, marginal climate where ripening is
  barely complete. The annual cycle is compressed and risky,
  which is one reason sparkling base wine is high in acid and
  low in alcohol — and why a warm, complete vintage stands out.

### Common misconceptions and corrections

- Véraison is not "the grapes are ripe". It is the *start* of
  ripening. Colour change in reds is visible, but sugar, acid,
  tannin, and flavour still have a long way to go.
- Flowering is not fruit set. Many flowers do not set, and
  set rate is weather-dependent. Do not treat "flowered" as
  "cropped".
- Dormancy is not dead. The vine is alive, respiring slowly,
  and vulnerable to extreme winter cold even without leaves.
- Harvest date is not ripeness. Ripeness is sugar, acid,
  flavour, and tannin maturity together. A high sugar reading
  alone does not mean a ripe grape.

### Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered:

1. During a grapevine's annual cycle, what happens at budburst,
   flowering, fruit set, and véraison? (Invite what the learner
   knows about any of these stages; do not presume all four.)
2. Why does a spring frost after budburst matter more than the
   same frost in mid-winter?
3. If flowering is cold and wet, what happens to yield, and why?
4. Why can two vineyards with the same variety ripen weeks apart?
5. What changes in the berry after véraison, and which change
   matters most for a winemaker deciding when to pick?

### Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If budburst/flowering/set blur together: draw the chain on a
  timeline and attach one weather risk to each link. Do not
  re-ask the same question in different words.
- If véraison is treated as ripeness: teach the post-véraison
  changes separately — sugar up, malic acid down, skins/seeds
  maturing, flavour building — and ask which one lags in a cool year.
- If harvest is treated as a calendar date: use a cool vs warm
  vintage for the same region and ask what the winemaker is
  trading off by waiting a week.

## Lesson 1.2 — The grape and vine needs

### Learning objectives

- State the vine's four needs: water, warmth, sunlight, nutrients —
  and what too little or too much of each does.
- Describe grape composition: sugars, acids (malic and tartaric),
  colour compounds, tannins, and flavour compounds; where each
  lives in the berry (pulp, skin, seed).
- Link ripeness to wine: potential alcohol, acidity, tannin
  maturity, flavour intensity and type.

### Introduction

A vine needs only four things, but it needs them in balance.
Water keeps the vine alive and moves nutrients; too much makes
big, dilute berries and vigorous leaf growth that shades fruit.
Warmth drives ripening; too little leaves sugar low and acid
high, too much burns acid and flavour away. Sunlight powers
photosynthesis and helps colour and flavour develop in skins;
too much direct heat can sunburn berries. Nutrients, mainly from
soil, support growth; excess nitrogen, like excess water, pushes
leaf growth at the expense of fruit quality.

Inside the berry, those needs become chemistry. Sugars accumulate
in the pulp and become alcohol in fermentation. Acids — malic,
which falls sharply as grapes ripen, and tartaric, which is more
stable — give freshness and structure. Colour lives mostly in
skins (for reds); tannin lives in skins and seeds. Flavour
compounds build late, and they, more than sugar, are what separate
a technically ripe grape from a flavour-ripe one.

Ripeness is therefore not one number. A grape can be sugar-ripe
and still have green tannins and undeveloped flavour. A winemaker
is balancing sugar against acid, tannin, and flavour — and that
balance is set by climate, site, and the season's weather.

### Core mechanisms, causally

- **Sugar → alcohol:** more sugar at harvest means higher
  potential alcohol, roughly. Warmth and sunlight raise sugar;
  cool conditions and early harvest lower it.
- **Malic acid → freshness:** malic acid is respired away as
  berries warm and ripen. Cool climates and cool nights preserve
  it; warm climates lose it. That is why a cool-climate white
  can taste crisp at the same sugar as a warm-climate white
  that tastes soft.
- **Tartaric acid → stability:** tartaric acid falls less with
  ripening, so it provides a floor of acidity even in warm regions.
- **Skin and seed maturity → tannin quality:** unripe tannins
  taste green, bitter, and harsh; ripe tannins taste softer and
  more integrated, even at the same quantity. Colour and tannin
  extraction in red winemaking depends on this maturity.
- **Water stress, mild vs severe:** mild water stress after
  véraison can be beneficial — smaller berries, more
  skin-to-juice, more concentration. Severe stress stops
  ripening, shuts the vine down, and can damage leaves and fruit.
  The difference is degree and timing, not "stress is good" or
  "stress is bad" in the abstract.
- **Root-zone oxygen:** roots need air as well as water.
  Waterlogged soil suffocates roots, even if the vine is not
  "thirsty". Good drainage is therefore about air, not just
  about removing excess water.

### Concrete examples

- **Cool-climate Riesling (Mosel):** high acidity retained,
  moderate alcohol, flavour built slowly. The wine's freshness
  is malic acid preserved by coolness, not acid added later.
- **Warm-climate Grenache (southern Rhône):** high sugar,
  high alcohol, lower acidity. The challenge is freshness, not
  ripeness.
- **Nebbiolo (Piedmont):** late ripening, high tannin and acid.
  It needs a long, warm autumn to mature tannin; picked early,
  it is harsh not because tannin is high in amount alone, but
  because it is unripe.

### Common misconceptions and corrections

- "More water = better grapes." No: excess water gives vigour,
  shading, dilution, and disease. Mild stress can improve quality.
- "High sugar = ripe." No: sugar, acid, tannin, and flavour
  ripen at different rates. Sugar is the easiest to measure,
  not the whole story.
- "Acidity is one thing." No: malic and tartaric behave
  differently. Malic falls with warmth; tartaric is steadier.
  That distinction explains many cool vs warm differences.
- "Tannin amount = tannin harshness." No: maturity matters.
  Ripe tannin can be abundant and still feel fine-grained.

### Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME

1. What four things does a vine need, and what happens when
   each is in excess?
2. Why does a cool climate preserve acidity better than a warm
   climate, and which acid is most affected?
3. What is the difference between mild and severe water stress
   after véraison, and what does each do to berry size and
   ripening?
4. Why does good drainage matter even when a vine has enough water?
5. A grape measures high in sugar but tastes green and harsh.
   What is unripe, and why?

### Deeper teaching notes

- If water stress is treated as simply good or bad: teach the
  mild/severe distinction with berry size and skin-to-juice
  ratio, then ask about timing — stress before véraison vs after.
- If root-zone oxygen is missed: contrast a free-draining
  gravel soil with a waterlogged clay in the same rainfall, and
  ask what the roots experience in each.
- If acidity is treated as a single number: split malic and
  tartaric, then revisit a cool white and a warm red and ask
  which acid story fits each.

## Lesson 1.3 — Regional climates

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish cool, moderate, warm, and hot climates by their
  effect on ripening, not just by average temperature.
- Distinguish maritime, continental, and Mediterranean climates
  by mechanism (ocean moderation, inland extremes, summer-dry
  seasonality) and by risk profile.
- Explain latitude, altitude, oceans/lakes, and diurnal range as
  modifiers of climate, not as climates themselves.
- Connect each climate pattern to grape composition and wine style.

### Introduction

Climate is the long-term pattern; weather is this year's version
of it. For vines, the useful question is not "what is the average
temperature?" but "how much warmth, when, with how much water,
and with how much swing between day and night and between
seasons?"

Cool climates barely ripen grapes. Wines tend to be lighter,
higher in acid, lower in alcohol, with more delicate, sometimes
green-edged flavours if ripening is incomplete. Moderate climates
ripen most varieties comfortably, with balance. Warm climates
ripen easily, with higher sugar and alcohol, lower acid, and
riper, sometimes cooked flavours. Hot climates risk losing
freshness altogether and need varieties and techniques that
preserve it.

On top of that scale sit three patterns, defined by water and
seasonality as much as by temperature. Maritime climates are
smoothed by the ocean. Continental climates swing with the
inland seasons. Mediterranean climates are dry in summer and
wet in winter. Each pattern brings a different main risk, and
that risk shapes viticulture more than the average temperature
alone.

### Maritime climates

**Mechanism:** large water bodies store heat and release it
slowly, moderating temperature. Summers are cooler, winters
milder, diurnal range is smaller, and rain is spread through
the year.

**Viticultural consequences:** slower, steadier ripening;
acidity retained; less winter kill and less extreme frost than
inland, but more humidity and disease pressure during the
growing season. Canopy management, airflow, and site drainage
matter a great deal. Vintage variation exists, but it is more
often about rain and disease at harvest than about extreme cold.

**Examples:** Bordeaux (Atlantic influence, with the Gironde
and rivers moderating further); coastal California where fog
and ocean winds cool the season; parts of the Loire near the
Atlantic.

### Continental climates

**Mechanism:** far from moderating water, the land heats and
cools quickly. Summers are hot, winters cold, diurnal range is
large, and rainfall is often concentrated in summer or is lower
overall, depending on the region.

**Viticultural consequences:** good summer ripening and cool
nights that can preserve acidity, but real winter cold that can
damage vines, and spring frost risk after budburst. Lower
humidity usually means less constant disease pressure than a
maritime climate, but vintage variation is high: a cold winter,
a frost spring, or a short summer changes the year sharply.
Site choice — slope, aspect, and cold-air drainage — becomes
critical, because cold air flows downhill and pools in hollows.

**Examples:** Burgundy (semi-continental: inland, with
continental swings softened by latitude and local factors);
Alsace (continental, sheltered by the Vosges from Atlantic rain,
with dry, sunny summers and cold winters); Champagne (cool and
continental-influenced, marginal for ripening); Piedmont
(continental influence inland from the Mediterranean, with fog
and diurnal range in the hills).

### Mediterranean climates

**Mechanism:** a seasonal rainfall pattern, not just warmth.
Winters are mild and wet; summers are warm to hot and dry.
The vine's water comes largely in winter, and summer is a long
dry ripening period.

**Viticultural consequences:** reliable ripening, high sugar
and potential alcohol, falling acidity, and flavour that can
become ripe and warm. The main risks are drought, heat, and
sunburn, not disease from summer rain. Water management —
winter soil storage, drought-tolerant rootstocks, irrigation
where allowed, canopy shade — is central. In very hot
Mediterranean sites, the challenge is preserving freshness,
sometimes by picking earlier, by altitude, or by varieties
that retain acid.

**Examples:** southern Rhône, Provence, southern Spain,
Sicily, and parts of California with a Mediterranean rainfall
pattern (wet winter, dry summer), though California's coastal
fog adds a maritime overlay in places.

### Latitude, altitude, oceans, diurnal range — modifiers

- **Latitude:** higher latitude means lower sun angle and a
  shorter, cooler growing season, but longer summer days.
  Lower latitude means more warmth and a longer season.
  Latitude sets the baseline; local factors move a site off it.
- **Altitude:** temperature falls with altitude (roughly
  0.6°C per 100 m, as a rule of thumb, varying locally).
  Higher sites can therefore ripen later and preserve acidity,
  and can sit above fog or frost pockets — but they also face
  greater cold, wind, and frost risk in exposed positions.
  Altitude is not automatically safer from frost.
- **Oceans and lakes:** water moderates temperature and can
  bring fog, humidity, and rain, or, in a rain shadow, dryness
  on the lee side. A lake can moderate a local climate and
  also create autumn fog that helps noble rot in sweet-wine
  regions — a useful contrast: fog as a problem in one place
  and a tool in another.
- **Diurnal range:** the difference between day and night
  temperature. Large diurnal range (hot days, cool nights)
  helps ripening by day while preserving acid by night, and
  can help colour and aroma. It is not the same as fog:
  morning fog burning off does not itself create diurnal range,
  though a clear, dry night after a hot day does.

### Concrete comparisons

- **Bordeaux vs Burgundy:** both in France, different patterns.
  Bordeaux's Atlantic moderation gives steadier ripening and
  rain/disease as a recurring harvest concern. Burgundy's inland
  position gives larger swings, frost and vintage variation as
  the recurring story, and a stronger role for slope and aspect.
- **Mosel vs southern Rhône:** Mosel is cool and marginal,
  with steep slate slopes capturing sun and heat to ripen
  Riesling at all. The southern Rhône is warm and Mediterranean,
  ripening Grenache easily and struggling to keep freshness.
  The same question — "is it ripe?" — has opposite answers.
- **Etna (Sicily):** Mediterranean latitude, but altitude and
  volcanic soils create a cooler, later-ripening site than the
  island's lowlands. Latitude alone would mislead; altitude
  and aspect rewrite the site's climate.

### Common misconceptions and corrections

- "Continental means dry." Not necessarily. Continental means
  large seasonal swings. Rainfall amount depends on local
  geography, mountains, and rain shadows.
- "Higher altitude always means less frost." No. Cold air
  drains downhill and pools in frost pockets. A mid-slope site
  can be safer than a valley floor or an exposed summit.
- "Fog = diurnal range." No. Fog is a moisture and visibility
  phenomenon. Diurnal range is a temperature difference.
  Fog can accompany a large diurnal range, but it does not
  create it.
- "Mediterranean = hot and dry all year." No. Mediterranean
  means dry summers and wet winters. Winter rain is part of
  the pattern, and soil water storage from winter rain matters
  for the dry summer.
- "Maritime = rainy and bad." No. Maritime moderation can be
  ideal for varieties that need a long, steady season, and
  rain is only a problem when it arrives at flowering or harvest
  or when it drives disease.

### Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME

**The next question, per STATE.md, is the first one below.
Ask it, wait for the answer, and deepen from gaps before moving on.**

1. Which of these three climate types — maritime, continental,
   Mediterranean — is most at risk from spring frost, which from
   rain-related disease in the growing season, and which from
   summer drought, and why in each case?
2. Why does a continental climate tend to show more vintage
   variation than a maritime climate in the same latitude band?
3. A vineyard at altitude ripens later than its valley neighbour.
   What has altitude changed, and what new risk has it introduced?
4. Why can two sites at the same latitude produce wines of very
   different ripeness and acidity?
5. How does a large diurnal range help both ripening and acidity
   retention, and why is that not the same as fog?

### Deeper teaching notes

- If climate types blur: anchor each to one mechanism (ocean
  moderation, inland swing, summer-dry seasonality) and one
  main risk (disease, frost/vintage variation, drought/heat),
  then test with an unfamiliar region before adding detail.
- If frost/topography is shaky: teach cold-air drainage and
  frost pockets explicitly, with a slope diagram in words —
  cold air flows down, pools in hollows, mid-slope is often
  safest — and correct any claim that elevation alone protects.
- If Mediterranean is reduced to "hot": re-teach the seasonal
  rainfall pattern and winter soil storage, then ask what
  happens if winter rain fails.

## Lesson 1.4 — Site and vintage

### Learning objectives

- Explain aspect, slope, and soil in terms of sun, heat, water,
  and air, not as labels.
- Distinguish climate (long-term pattern) from weather and
  vintage (this year's conditions).
- Explain how site can override regional climate, in both directions.

### Introduction

Region gives you the climate. Site gives you the vineyard's
actual experience of it. Two vineyards a kilometre apart can
ripen differently because one faces south and the other faces
north, one is on a slope that drains cold air and the other is
in a hollow that collects it, one soil holds water and the other
lets it drain away.

Aspect is which way a slope faces. In the northern hemisphere,
south-facing slopes receive more direct sun and warmth; north-
facing slopes are cooler. Slope itself affects drainage of both
water and cold air, and steep slopes can capture sun at a better
angle. Soil affects water availability, drainage, root depth, and
vigour — and through vigour, shading and yield — more directly
than it affects flavour by some mysterious mineral transfer.
That last point matters: the WSET view is that soil influences
wine mainly through water, vigour, and ripening, not by vines
"drinking" flavour from rocks.

Vintage is the year's weather laid over the site. A warm, dry
vintage ripens earlier and fuller; a cool, wet vintage ripens
later and leaner. In a marginal climate, vintage differences are
large. In a warm, consistent climate, they are smaller — but
they do not disappear, because rain at harvest or a heat spike
can still change a year.

### Core mechanisms, causally

- **Aspect → warmth and ripening:** south-facing (northern
  hemisphere) = more sun, earlier ripening, higher sugar.
  In hot climates, a cooler aspect can be an advantage.
- **Slope → drainage and air:** slopes drain water and cold
  air. Valley floors collect both, and can be frost pockets
  and waterlogged. Steep slopes also warm by capturing sun.
- **Soil → water and vigour:** deep, water-retentive soils
  support vigour and yield, and can dilute flavour in wet years.
  Free-draining, poorer soils stress vines mildly, reduce vigour,
  and can concentrate fruit — if water does not become severely
  limiting.
- **Fog → two stories:** coastal or river fog cools and delays
  ripening, and can bring disease pressure. Autumn fog in a
  sweet-wine region (Sauternes/Barsac, Tokaj) can promote noble
  rot, Botrytis cinerea, which concentrates sugars and adds
  distinctive flavours. Same phenomenon, opposite judgement,
  depending on timing and purpose.
- **Weather vs climate:** climate is the pattern over decades;
  weather is this season. A single warm year does not make a
  region warm, and a single frost does not make it continental.
  Vintage is weather, experienced through climate and site.

### Concrete examples

- **Côte de Nuits, Burgundy:** an east-facing slope, limestone
  soils, mid-slope sites prized. Aspect, slope, and drainage
  together explain why a premier cru site a few metres from a
  village site can ripen differently.
- **Sauternes/Barsac:** morning fog from the Ciron and Garonne,
  followed by warm afternoons, creates conditions for noble rot.
  Without that fog pattern, the same grapes would make dry wine.
- **Mosel:** steep, south-facing slate slopes capture sun in a
  cool climate, making ripening possible where flat land would fail.
- **Etna:** altitude and aspect on volcanic slopes create cooler
  sites within a Mediterranean island, with later ripening and
  fresher wines than the lowlands.

### Common misconceptions and corrections

- "Soil flavour goes directly into wine." The supported
  explanation is indirect: soil affects water, vigour, root
  depth, and ripening, which affect grape composition, which
  affects wine. Be sceptical of direct mineral-flavour claims.
- "A good vintage means every wine is good." No. Vintage
  favours some varieties, sites, and styles more than others.
  A warm year helps a late-ripening variety and can hurt a
  variety that loses acid quickly.
- "Site doesn't matter in a warm climate." It matters
  differently: aspect, altitude, and water become tools to
  preserve freshness, not just to achieve ripeness.

### Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME

1. Why is a mid-slope site often safer from frost than a valley
   floor, even though it is higher?
2. How can soil affect wine quality without transferring flavour
   directly from rock to grape?
3. What is the difference between climate, weather, and vintage,
   in one sentence each?
4. Why can fog be a problem in one region and a benefit in
   Sauternes or Tokaj?
5. A cool-climate site and a warm-climate site both have a wet
   harvest. Why is the consequence likely to be worse in the
   cool site?

### Deeper teaching notes

- If frost pockets are confused: return to cold-air drainage
  as a physical flow, not a temperature label, and ask the
  learner to place three sites — summit, mid-slope, hollow —
  in frost-risk order.
- If soil-flavour claims persist: ask for a mechanism. If no
  mechanism via water/vigour/ripening is offered, the claim is
  not yet a WSET explanation.

## Lesson 1.5 — Integration and checkpoint

### Purpose

This lesson is not new teaching. It tests whether Lessons 1.1–1.4
can be used together, on unfamiliar ground, without guidance.
Do not run it until the essential topics above have each been
addressed at least once. Do not count guided answers as a pass.

### Integrated scenario (use one, not both, for the unaided test)

**Scenario A — Two unfamiliar vineyards.**
Vineyard A is inland, on a mid-slope, south-facing, with
free-draining soil, a large diurnal range, and a dry summer.
Vineyard B is coastal, on flat land, with water-retentive soil,
small diurnal range, and rain through the growing season.
Both grow the same variety. The learner should predict, with
reasons: which ripens earlier, which retains more acidity,
which faces greater disease risk, which faces greater frost
risk, and what style difference to expect in the wine — and
should identify where the prediction is uncertain and why.

**Scenario B — One vintage, two outcomes.**
A cool region has a warm, dry vintage after two cool, wet ones.
The learner should explain, with reasons, what changes in
sugar, acid, tannin, and flavour; which varieties benefit most
and which least; and why a warm vintage does not automatically
mean a better wine for every producer in the region.

### Checkpoint — recall check (about 8–10 questions, unaided)

1. Name the stages of the annual cycle in order, from dormancy
   to leaf fall.
2. What happens at véraison, and what has not yet happened?
3. Which acid falls most with ripening, and why does that matter
   for cool vs warm climates?
4. What is the difference between mild and severe water stress?
5. Why does good drainage matter for roots, beyond removing water?
6. Distinguish maritime, continental, and Mediterranean climates
   by mechanism, not just by temperature.
7. Why is a valley floor often more frost-prone than a mid-slope?
8. What is the difference between climate, weather, and vintage?
