## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

The Loire is a river, not a single climate. It runs from the Atlantic inland, and as it goes, the climate becomes more continental, the grapes change, and the wines change with them. Near the Atlantic, Muscadet is light, high in acid, and made to drink young, often on lees for texture. In the centre, Sauvignon Blanc makes crisp, mineral, citrus wines in Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé, on limestone and flint soils. Further along, Chenin Blanc makes everything from dry to sweet, sparkling to still, because its high acidity survives ripening and even noble rot. Alsace, by contrast, is a single, sheltered valley: continental, dry, and sunny, with a rain shadow from the Vosges, where wines are labelled by grape variety and range from dry to lusciously sweet. Together, these two regions test whether a learner can use climate and grape logic, rather than memorising appellation lists.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Loire climate gradient: maritime in the west (cool, wet, high acid, light wines) to continental in the centre and east (warmer summers, colder winters, more ripeness, more vintage variation).
- Sauvignon Blanc: aromatic, high acid, grassy/gooseberry/citrus, best when ripeness is balanced — too cool and it is green and harsh, too warm and it loses freshness.
- Chenin Blanc: high acid, versatile, capable of dry, off-dry, sweet, and sparkling styles. Acidity is what allows sweet Chenin to age and stay fresh.
- Muscadet and lees: Melon de Bourgogne is neutral in flavour, so lees ageing (sur lie) adds texture and a slight yeasty character that the grape alone would not give.
- Alsace rain shadow: the Vosges block Atlantic rain, making Alsace one of France's driest, sunniest regions despite its northern latitude. That dryness and sun allow Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris, and Muscat to ripen fully in a continental climate.
- Alsace varietal labelling: wines are usually named for the grape, not the place, which makes comparison by grape easier — but sweetness is not always obvious from the label, and Vendanges Tardives (late harvest) and Sélection de Grains Nobles (noble rot selection) are specific sweet categories.

## Concrete examples

- Sancerre vs New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc (for contrast): Sancerre is typically more mineral, restrained, and citrus-driven; New Zealand is more pungent and tropical. Same grape, different climate and winemaking.
- Vouvray: Chenin Blanc from dry (sec) to sweet (moelleux), still and sparkling, from the same appellation — a lesson in how one grape and one place can produce a whole range, driven by ripeness and botrytis.
- Muscadet sur lie: a light, neutral white given texture by lees, traditionally paired with seafood — a winemaking choice compensating for a neutral grape.
- Alsace Riesling vs Mosel Riesling: Alsace Riesling is typically drier, fuller, and more alcoholic, from a drier, sunnier continental site; Mosel Riesling is lighter, lower in alcohol, and often off-dry, from a cooler, marginal site.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Loire white is all Sauvignon Blanc.' No: Chenin Blanc and Melon de Bourgogne are equally central, and Cabernet Franc makes important Loire reds.
- 'Muscadet is always simple.' Sur lie Muscadet from good producers has texture and ageing potential, and the best examples are not thin or dilute.
- 'Alsace wines are all sweet.' No: most Alsace wine is dry or off-dry. Sweetness varies by producer and by grape (Gewurztraminer is often richer and sweeter in impression than Riesling, even at the same residual sugar).
- 'Flint soil gives a flinty taste directly.' The supported explanation is indirect: soil affects drainage, warmth, and ripening. 'Minerality' as a direct soil flavour is not a WSET mechanism.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. How does the Loire's climate change from west to east, and how do its grapes change with it?
2. Why can Chenin Blanc make dry, sweet, still, and sparkling wines, while many grapes cannot?
3. What does 'sur lie' add to Muscadet, and why does that grape need it?
4. How does the Vosges rain shadow shape Alsace, despite its northern latitude?
5. Why is sweetness harder to predict from an Alsace label than from a German label?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If Loire is reduced to Sancerre: teach the west-to-east gradient as a journey, and ask the learner to place Muscadet, Vouvray, and Sancerre on it before adding detail.
- If Alsace sweetness confuses: separate grape character (Gewurztraminer's richness) from residual sugar, and teach Vendanges Tardives and Sélection de Grains Nobles as production categories, not as sweetness levels alone.
