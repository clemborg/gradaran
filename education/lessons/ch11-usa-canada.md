## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

California is not one climate, and the fastest way to understand it is to follow the Pacific. The California Current brings cold water down the coast, fog rolls in, and the Coast Ranges block or channel that cooling. Where fog and ocean winds reach, Pinot Noir and Chardonnay thrive, cool and high in acid. Where inland valleys heat up, Cabernet Sauvignon, Zinfandel, and Syrah ripen fully and powerfully. That coastal-inland gradient, plus altitude and valley orientation, explains Napa vs Sonoma, Anderson Valley vs Ukiah, and Santa Barbara's east-west valleys, which funnel ocean air inland in a way north-south valleys do not. Oregon and Washington add a second logic: Oregon's Willamette Valley is cool and maritime, suited to Pinot Noir; Washington's Columbia Valley is continental, dry, and sunny, with irrigation, suited to Cabernet, Merlot, and Syrah. Canada adds a third: cool, marginal, with icewine as a special case of frozen grapes and concentrated sweetness.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Pacific cooling: the cold California Current, fog, and onshore winds cool coastal sites, delay ripening, and preserve acidity. Inland sites, shielded by ranges, are warmer and ripen earlier and fuller.
- Valley orientation: east-west valleys (Santa Maria, Santa Ynez) channel ocean air inland, cooling sites far from the coast. North-south valleys (Napa, Sonoma) have a gradient from cool south to warm north, with Carneros cool and Calistoga warm.
- Diurnal range in California: large inland diurnal range (hot days, cool nights) helps ripening by day and acid retention by night, especially in Napa and Paso Robles.
- Willamette Valley: cool, maritime, with Pinot Noir as the signature grape — thin-skinned, early-ripening, suited to a cool climate, with cherry, earth, and, with age, forest-floor notes.
- Columbia Valley: continental, dry, sunny, with irrigation from the Columbia River system. Low rainfall means disease pressure is low, but water must be managed. Cabernet, Merlot, and Syrah ripen fully with structure.
- Icewine: grapes frozen on the vine (at least -8°C in Canada), pressed while frozen so water stays as ice and sugar, acid, and flavour concentrate. It is a climate gamble, not a winemaking trick, and it requires a cold, reliable winter.

## Concrete examples

- Carneros vs Calistoga: Carneros, at Napa's cool southern end, straddling Napa and Sonoma, suits Pinot Noir and Chardonnay and sparkling base wine; Calistoga, at the warm northern end, suits Cabernet Sauvignon. Same valley, opposite ends, opposite grapes — a lesson in gradient, not in 'Napa style'.
- Anderson Valley vs Ukiah: Anderson Valley, near the coast, is cool and fog-influenced, suited to Pinot Noir and sparkling wine; Ukiah, inland, is warmer and suits other varieties. Latitude alone would mislead; coastal proximity and valley shape matter more.
- Santa Barbara: east-west valleys funnel ocean air, so Pinot Noir and Chardonnay thrive far inland, in a way they would not in a north-south valley at the same latitude.
- Walla Walla vs Willamette: Walla Walla is continental, warm, and structured for Cabernet and Syrah; Willamette is cool, maritime, and elegant for Pinot Noir. Same country, opposite logics.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Napa is all Cabernet.' Napa grows Cabernet famously, but Carneros grows Pinot and Chardonnay, and the valley's range includes Merlot, Syrah, and Zinfandel. 'Napa style' is a simplification.
- 'California wine is all big and alcoholic.' Coastal, cool-climate California — Sonoma Coast, Santa Cruz Mountains, Anderson Valley — makes elegant, high-acid wines. Bigness is a warm-site and winemaking choice, not a state law.
- 'Los Carneros is in Napa.' Los Carneros AVA spans both Napa and Sonoma counties. Do not present it as Napa-only.
- 'Icewine is made by freezing grapes in a freezer.' True icewine is frozen on the vine by natural cold. Freezer-made 'icewine' is not the same product and is not permitted as icewine in Canada.

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If California is treated as one style: use the Carneros-Calisto gradient and the Anderson-Ukiah contrast as two separate gradients, and ask the learner to place a new AVA on each before adding detail.
- If AVA is confused with European appellation law: teach AVA as a geographic boundary with a varietal percentage rule, not as a grape, yield, or winemaking law in the European sense.
