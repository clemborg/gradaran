## Learning objectives

- Place the northern Rhône: Côte-Rôtie, Hermitage, Crozes-Hermitage, Saint-Joseph, Condrieu, and their steep, continental-influenced sites.
- Explain northern Syrah and Viognier: what each gives, why Viognier is co-fermented in Côte-Rôtie, and why Condrieu is Viognier alone.
- Place the southern Rhône: Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Gigondas, Vacqueyras, Côtes du Rhône, and their Mediterranean climate and galets roulés soils.
- Explain southern blends: Grenache, Syrah, Mourvèdre (GSM) and others, and why blending is essential in a hot climate.
- Explain southern France beyond the Rhône: Provence rosé, Languedoc-Roussillon, and their value and style logic.

## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

The Rhône river runs from cool to warm, and its wines follow it. In the north, the valley is narrow and steep, the climate is continental with Mediterranean influence, and the hero is Syrah — dark, peppery, tannic, and ageworthy — grown on granite slopes so steep they must be terraced. In the south, the valley opens out, the climate becomes fully Mediterranean, and the hero becomes Grenache — warm, ripe, high in alcohol, lower in tannin and colour — blended with Syrah and Mourvèdre to give it structure and depth. That north-south contrast, plus the famous rolled pebbles (galets roulés) of Châteauneuf-du-Pape that store daytime heat and radiate it at night, is the chapter. Southern France beyond the Rhône — Provence rosé, Languedoc reds and whites — extends the same Mediterranean logic to larger, more varied, and often better-value wines.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Northern Rhône climate and slope: continental, with cold winters and warm summers, steep granite slopes that capture sun and drain well, and the mistral wind that can cool and dry the vines.
- Syrah: thick skins, high tannin, dark fruit, black pepper, and, with age, savoury, meaty, and olive notes. It needs warmth to ripen fully, and in a cool site it can be lean and peppery; in a warm site, rich and dark.
- Viognier: aromatic, low acid, apricot and floral. In Côte-Rôtie, a small amount co-fermented with Syrah stabilises colour and adds perfume; in Condrieu, alone, it makes full, aromatic, low-acid whites that must be drunk young.
- Southern Rhône blending: Grenache ripens easily and gives alcohol and red fruit, but lacks tannin and colour; Syrah adds tannin, colour, and spice; Mourvèdre adds structure, dark fruit, and ageing potential, but ripens late and needs warmth. No single variety does the whole job in this climate.
- Galets roulés: large, rounded stones that absorb heat by day and release it at night, helping ripening in a climate that already ripens easily — useful, but not magic, and not the only soil in Châteauneuf-du-Pape, which also has sand, clay, and limestone.

## Concrete examples

- Hermitage vs Crozes-Hermitage: Hermitage, from the hill itself, is structured and ageworthy; Crozes, from the surrounding plains, is lighter and earlier-drinking. Same grape, different site.
- Châteauneuf-du-Pape: a blend of up to 13 varieties (in practice, mainly Grenache, Syrah, Mourvèdre), full, warm, and spicy, with the galets roulés story as a memorable but partial explanation.
- Provence rosé: pale, dry, delicate rosé made by direct pressing or brief skin contact, in a Mediterranean climate — a style defined by intention (pale, fresh, food-friendly) rather than by a single grape.
- Condrieu: Viognier alone, aromatic and full, low in acid, best young — a lesson in how a grape's acid level limits its ageing and food-pairing range.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Châteauneuf-du-Pape is all galets roulés.' No: galets are famous, but the appellation has varied soils, and the wine's character comes from climate, blend, and winemaking as much as from stones.
- 'Northern and southern Rhône are the same region.' They share a river, not a climate, grape, or style. Treating them as one is the most common Rhône error.
- 'Grenache is always light and simple.' Grenache can be light, but old-vine, low-yield Grenache in the southern Rhône and in Spain makes concentrated, serious wines.
- 'Rosé is a by-product.' Provence rosé is deliberately made, often by direct pressing, to a pale, dry style. Saignée (bleeding off juice from a red fermentation) is a different method, with different results.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. Why is Syrah the northern Rhône grape and Grenache the southern Rhône grape, in climate terms?
2. What does co-fermenting Viognier with Syrah do in Côte-Rôtie, and why is it not done everywhere?
3. Why must southern Rhône reds usually be blends, and what does each GSM variety contribute?
4. What do galets roulés actually do, and what do they not do?
5. How does Provence rosé differ in method and intention from a saignée rosé?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If north/south blur: draw the river as a temperature gradient and place one appellation and one grape at each end before adding the middle.
- If galets are treated as magic: ask what would happen if the same stones were in a cool climate — the answer (little, without warmth to store) shows stones are amplifiers, not creators, of heat.
