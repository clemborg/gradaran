## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

By this chapter, the learner should not need new facts. They need to use the facts they have, under pressure, in the forms WSET actually asks for. A multiple-choice question tests recall and discrimination: can you tell the right answer from a plausible distractor? A short written answer tests causal reasoning: can you explain why, not just what, in a structured paragraph? A blind tasting tests observation and conclusion: can you describe what is in the glass, in SAT order, and draw a quality and readiness conclusion that follows from the description, not from a guess at the label? This chapter therefore has no new region to learn. It has practice, timing, and honest audit: what is actually known, what is only exposed, and what still needs review before a course in early 2027 and an exam by April 2027.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Short-answer structure: state the condition, explain the mechanism, link to grape composition or production, and conclude with style, quality, or price. A good short answer is a chain, not a list. Example chain: cool climate → slow ripening → retained malic acid → high-acid, low-alcohol wine → needs food or ageing, or suits sparkling base wine.
- Multiple-choice strategy: eliminate distractors by mechanism, not by familiarity. If two options differ only in a mechanism (for example, fortification timing), the mechanism is the test.
- SAT structure: appearance (clarity, intensity, colour), nose (condition, intensity, aroma characteristics, development), palate (sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity and characteristics, finish), conclusions (quality, readiness for drinking/potential for ageing). Each section must follow from observation, not from a guessed label.
- Blind tasting discipline: describe first, conclude second. Do not write 'Burgundy' in the appearance section. Quality is judged by balance, length, intensity, and complexity, not by price or prestige.
- Spaced review: topics that failed a checkpoint or an audit return at ~1 week and ~3 weeks, with new examples, not the same questions. A topic that passes twice, in unfamiliar cases, with a delay, is closer to mastered; a topic that passes once, with guidance, is not.

## Concrete examples

- A short answer on why Mosel Riesling is low in alcohol and high in acid: cool climate, slow ripening, malic acid retained, sugar low, fermentation often stopped or naturally low — a chain, not 'because it is German'.
- A short answer on why Barolo needs ageing: Nebbiolo's high tannin and acid, late ripening, tannin maturity lagging sugar, and bottle time softening tannin and developing tertiary aromas.
- A blind tasting note: a pale, high-acid white with citrus and green apple, light body, short finish — a quality conclusion of good, drink now, not outstanding, because intensity and complexity are limited, even if the wine is clean and balanced.
- A comparison: two Pinots, one from Burgundy, one from Central Otago — same grape, different climate, site, and winemaking, leading to different tannin, acid, and aroma, and different ageing.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'I know this region because I have tasted it.' Tasting experience is exposure, not theory mastery, and not SAT mastery. Both must be evidenced separately.
- 'A long answer is a good answer.' In short-answer exams, a long list without a causal chain scores less than a short chain with a mechanism. Length is not depth.
- 'Blind tasting is guessing the label.' Blind tasting is describing and concluding on quality and readiness. A correct label guess with a poor description is not a pass.
- 'Revision means re-reading notes.' Revision means testing recall, in new cases, after a delay. Re-reading without testing feels productive and is often not.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. Write a short answer: why does a cool climate preserve acidity, and what does that mean for wine style? (Then check: did the answer include a mechanism, not just a claim?)
2. Compare two regions you have studied, in causal form, without using a list of facts alone.
3. Describe a white wine using SAT order, from appearance to conclusions, without naming a region or grape in the description sections.
4. What is the difference between a good and an outstanding wine, in SAT quality terms?
5. Which topics from your last audit are due for spaced review, and what new example will you use to test them?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If short answers are lists: rewrite one as a chain, with arrows — condition → mechanism → composition → style — and ask which link is missing.
- If tasting notes name the wine early: re-teach SAT order as a discipline — observe, then conclude — and practise with a wine whose label is hidden, with the conclusion checked against the description, not against the label.

**Two causal explanations (largely independent, unaided):**

1. Explain how a single mechanism — for example, altitude — can lead to different wine styles in two different regions, and why the mechanism alone does not predict the wine.
2. Explain how a winemaking decision — for example, MLF — leads to different styles from the same grape, and what trade-off it involves.

**One new comparison/application scenario (unaided, no material conceptual mistake):**

A learner is given a new, unfamiliar region and grape, with only climate, soil, and winemaking information, and no tasting. The learner should predict, with reasons, the likely style, quality factors, and price logic, and state what tasting information would confirm or overturn the prediction. This is the ultimate integration test: can the system be used, not just recalled?
