## Learning objectives

- Explain training systems and pruning as decisions about light, air, yield, and labour, not as shapes to memorise.
- Explain canopy management: leaf removal, shoot positioning, and vigour control, and their effects on ripening, disease, and flavour.
- Explain yield: how it is set, why lower yield can concentrate flavour, and why 'lower is always better' is false.
- Explain irrigation, harvest timing, and mechanical vs hand harvesting, with trade-offs.
- Explain weather hazards (frost, hail, rain, heat), pests and diseases, and sustainable responses, including organic and biodynamic approaches without overstating their effects.

## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

A vineyard is not a wild plant. Every winter, someone decides how many buds to leave. Every summer, someone decides how much leaf to keep, whether to water, and when to pick. Those decisions are not decoration: they change how much sunlight reaches the fruit, how much air moves through the canopy, how many bunches the vine must ripen, and therefore how concentrated, ripe, and healthy the grapes are. A grower in a cool, wet climate and a grower in a hot, dry climate can make opposite canopy decisions for the same reason — to get the fruit ripe and healthy — because their risks are opposite. Human factors are therefore best taught as responses to natural factors, not as a separate list.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Pruning sets bud number, and therefore potential yield and vigour. More buds mean more shoots and more fruit to ripen; fewer buds mean more concentration per bunch, but also less crop if frost or disease strikes.
- Canopy management controls light and air. Open canopies ripen fruit more evenly and dry faster after rain, reducing disease; too much leaf removal in a hot climate sunburns fruit and loses freshness.
- Yield and concentration: a vine has a limited photosynthetic capacity. Spreading it over many bunches dilutes sugar, colour, and flavour; concentrating it over few bunches can raise quality, but only up to a point, and at a cost in volume and therefore price.
- Water management: irrigation can save a crop in a dry climate and can also dilute it if used carelessly. Deficit irrigation — giving less than full water — is a tool to impose mild stress deliberately, after véraison, for concentration.
- Harvest timing is a human decision about ripeness trade-offs: sugar vs acid vs tannin vs flavour. Mechanical harvesting is fast and cheap and suits large, flat vineyards; hand harvesting is selective and gentle and suits steep, fragile, or premium sites.
- Pests and diseases: phylloxera (a root louse) reshaped world viticulture and is why most vines are grafted onto resistant rootstocks. Fungal diseases (downy and powdery mildew, grey rot) thrive in humidity; control is partly canopy, partly site, partly spray, and partly variety choice.

## Concrete examples

- Burgundy, Guyot pruning and low yields: a single cane system that limits buds, suits a cool climate where ripening is marginal and concentration matters.
- Mosel, steep slopes: hand harvesting is not a romantic choice but a physical necessity on slopes too steep for machines, and it allows selection of botrytised bunches for sweet wines.
- Southern Rhône, bush vines (gobelet): no trellis, low to the ground, suited to hot, dry, windy sites where shade from the vine's own canopy protects fruit.
- California, drip irrigation: precise water delivery in a summer-dry climate, allowing deficit irrigation and control of vigour that rain-fed regions cannot choose.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Organic means no spraying.' No: organic viticulture restricts which sprays are used (for example copper and sulphur), not whether disease is managed. It can still involve significant intervention.
- 'Lower yield always means better wine.' No: below a point, lower yield does not improve quality further, and it raises cost. The useful question is whether the vine can ripen its crop fully.
- 'Mechanical harvesting always damages quality.' No: modern machines are gentle enough for many wines, and speed at harvest can itself preserve quality by picking at the right moment. The trade-off is selectivity, not a moral judgement.
- 'Biodynamic is just organic with a calendar.' Biodynamic viticulture includes organic practices plus specific preparations and a philosophical framework. Its effects should be described accurately, without claiming unproven mechanisms as WSET facts.

## Diagnostic questions — ask ONE AT A TIME, in a live session

These are listed for the tutor to ask, not already asked or answered. Ask one, then wait for the answer before asking the next:

1. Why does pruning in winter affect the wine two seasons later, not just the next harvest?
2. A grower in a humid climate and a grower in a hot, dry climate both change their canopy. Why might they do opposite things?
3. What is the mechanism by which lower yield can improve concentration, and where does that logic stop?
4. When is mechanical harvesting the better choice, and when is hand harvesting worth its cost?
5. How did phylloxera change viticulture, and why are most vines grafted today?

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If pruning is treated as a shape: re-teach it as bud-number arithmetic — buds → shoots → bunches → ripening load — and ask what happens if frost removes half the buds.
- If organic/biodynamic blur: separate the three layers — what is sprayed, what is philosophically claimed, and what is evidenced — and keep WSET explanations to the first and third where possible.
