## Introduction (the 1-3 minute opening, in text)

Chile and Argentina are both long, narrow countries shaped by the same two forces: the Andes in the east and the Pacific in the west. Chile is a strip between ocean and mountains, with valleys running east-west from the Andes to the sea, so a vineyard's distance from the ocean and its altitude together set its climate. Argentina is on the other side of the Andes, in a rain shadow, dry and sunny, where altitude is the main tool for cooling: Mendoza's vineyards sit at 600 to 1100 metres, and Salta's at up to 3000 metres, among the highest in the world. In both countries, water is managed — irrigation is normal, not exceptional — because rainfall is low and sunshine is abundant. The wines that result are often ripe, fruity, and full, but with altitude and coastal influence, they can also be fresh and structured, especially Malbec at altitude and Sauvignon Blanc near the coast.

## Core mechanisms, causally

- Chilean valleys: east-west valleys channel Pacific cooling inland. Coastal sites (Casablanca, Leyda) are cool, foggy, and suited to Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay; inland sites (Maipo, Colchagua) are warmer and suited to Cabernet and Carménère.
- Carménère: a Bordeaux variety, long mistaken for Merlot in Chile, late-ripening, with green pepper notes if underripe and ripe, soft, plum and spice notes if fully ripe. Its history is a lesson in why variety identification matters.
- Argentine altitude: temperature falls with altitude, so high vineyards ripen later and preserve acidity despite strong sun and latitude. Diurnal range is large, helping colour and aroma while retaining freshness.
- Irrigation in dry climates: with low rainfall, irrigation controls water precisely, but it must be managed to avoid dilution. Deficit irrigation after véraison is used to concentrate fruit, as in other dry regions.
- Mendoza Malbec: thick skins, dark colour, plum, violet, and chocolate notes, with tannin that can be soft or structured depending on site and oak. At altitude, Malbec keeps acidity and floral notes; at lower, warmer sites, it becomes riper and jammier.
- Torrontés: aromatic, floral, Muscat-like, high in alcohol, low in acid, from Salta's high, sunny sites — a grape that would be flabby at low altitude but gains freshness from altitude and diurnal range.

## Concrete examples

- Casablanca Sauvignon Blanc vs Maipo Cabernet: coastal cool vs inland warm, from the same country, showing Chile's east-west gradient.
- Mendoza Malbec at 1000 m vs Salta Torrontés at 2000 m: altitude as a cooling tool in two different grapes and styles.
- Carménère: a variety that was misidentified for years, and whose green pepper note is a ripeness signal, not a varietal flaw — underripe Carménère tastes green, ripe Carménère tastes soft and spicy.
- Patagonia: cooler, windier, southern, with Pinot Noir and sparkling wine — a reminder that Argentina is not only Mendoza and Malbec.

## Common misconceptions and corrections

- 'Chilean wine is all cheap Cabernet.' Chile makes serious, ageworthy Cabernet, Carménère, and Syrah, especially from old vines and coastal sites, alongside value wines. Price tier is not a region definition.
- 'Malbec is a French grape, so Argentine Malbec should taste French.' Argentine Malbec, at altitude and in strong sun, is riper, darker, and more floral than Cahors Malbec, which is cooler and more structured. Same grape, different climate.
- 'Irrigation always dilutes.' Managed irrigation, especially deficit irrigation, can improve quality by controlling vigour and stress. Unmanaged over-watering dilutes; irrigation itself does not automatically do so.
- 'Altitude always means cool and elegant.' Altitude also means stronger UV, greater frost risk, and thinner air. It cools, but it also changes light and risk, not just temperature.

## Deeper teaching notes (use only where answers show gaps)

- If Chile/Argentina blur into 'South America': separate them by ocean vs rain shadow, and by grape — Carménère and Malbec as signatures — before adding subregions.
- If altitude is treated as only 'cooler': add UV, frost, and diurnal range, and ask what altitude does to colour and aroma, not just to temperature.
